Socrates’ final day, told by Phaedo: a dialogue on philosophy, the soul, and the meaning of death. Select a Stephanus reference to move through the text.

57a

Echecrates: Phaedo, were you there yourself with Socrates on the day he drank the poison in prison, or did you hear about it from someone else?

Phaedo: I was there myself, Echecrates.

Echecrates: Then what did he say before his death, and how did he meet it? I would be glad to hear. These days hardly anyone from Phlius goes to Athens, and no visitor has come from there for a long time—

57b

Echecrates (continuing): —who could give us a clear report of these events. We heard only that he died after drinking poison; no one could tell us anything else.

58a

Phaedo: So you did not even learn how the trial took place?

Echecrates: Yes, someone told us about that. We were surprised that he seems to have died much later, though the trial had taken place some time earlier. What was the reason, Phaedo?

Phaedo: A chance circumstance, Echecrates. It happened that on the day before the trial the stern of the ship the Athenians send to Delos had been garlanded.

Echecrates: What does that mean?

Phaedo: It is the ship in which, as the Athenians say, Theseus once went to Crete—

58b

Phaedo: —taking those famous seven pairs; he saved them and was saved himself. They say the Athenians then vowed to Apollo that, if they were saved, they would send a sacred delegation to Delos each year. From then until now they have sent it to the god every year, as they still do. Once they begin the sacred voyage, they have a law that during this period the city must remain pure and no one may be put to death by public authority until the ship has gone to Delos and returned here. Sometimes this takes a long time, whenever winds happen to hold—

58c

Phaedo (continuing): —them back. The sacred voyage begins when Apollo’s priest garlands the ship’s stern. That, as I say, happened by chance on the day before the trial. For this reason a long time passed for Socrates in prison between the trial and his death.

Echecrates: And what about the death itself, Phaedo? What was said and done, and which of his friends were there? Or did the officials not allow them to be present, so that he died without friends around him?

58d

Phaedo: Not at all. Some were there—in fact, many.

Echecrates: Then please make every effort to tell us all this as clearly as you can, unless you happen to be busy.

Phaedo: I am free, and I will try to tell you. Remembering Socrates, whether speaking myself or listening to someone else, is always the greatest pleasure for me.

58e

Echecrates: And you have other listeners like that, Phaedo. So try to go through everything as carefully as you can.

Phaedo: I had a remarkable experience while I was there. I felt no pity, as one might expect to feel when present at the death of a close friend. The man seemed happy to me, Echecrates, both in his character and in his words. He met his end so fearlessly and nobly that I felt he was not going to Hades without divine favor, and that once there he would fare well, as surely as anyone ever has.

59a

Phaedo (continuing): For that reason I felt no great sorrow, as one might reasonably expect at a time of mourning. Nor, on the other hand, did I feel the pleasure we usually feel when engaged in philosophy, as we were then, since our conversation was of that kind. Instead, I was gripped by a very strange feeling, an unusual mixture of pleasure and pain together, as I thought that he would soon die. Much the same was true of all of us there: at one moment we laughed, at another we wept. One of us in particular, Apollodorus—

59b

Echecrates: You know the man and his ways.

Echecrates: Of course.

Phaedo: He was completely overcome like that; and I myself was shaken, as were the others.

Echecrates: Who happened to be there, Phaedo?

Phaedo: Apollodorus was there, along with the local men Critobulus and his father, Hermogenes, Epigenes, Aeschines, and Antisthenes. Ctesippus of Paeania was there too, and Menexenus, and some others from around here. Plato, I think, was ill.

Echecrates: Were any visitors there?

59c

Phaedo: Yes: Simmias from Thebes, Cebes, Phaedondes, and the two men from Megara, Euclides and Terpsion.

Echecrates: What about Aristippus and Cleombrotus? Were they there?

Phaedo: No. They were said to be in Aegina.

Echecrates: Was anyone else there?

Phaedo: I think that was just about everyone.

Echecrates: So then, what do you say the conversation was?

Phaedo: I will try to tell you the whole story from the beginning.

59d

Phaedo (continuing): In the days before, it had been our custom for me and the others to visit Socrates, meeting early at the courthouse where the trial had taken place, since it was close to the prison. Each time we waited together until the prison opened; it did not open early. When it did open, we went in to Socrates and spent most of the day with him. That morning, too, we had gathered earlier than usual. For on the day before—

59e

Phaedo (continuing): —when we left the prison in the evening, we learned that the ship had arrived from Delos. So we told one another to come to our usual place as early as possible. We arrived, and the doorkeeper—the same man who usually answered the door—came out and told us to wait and not go in until he gave the word. “The Eleven are releasing Socrates,” he said, “and telling him that he is to die today.”

60a

Phaedo (continuing): He did not keep us waiting long. He came back and told us to go in. When we entered, we found Socrates just released from his chains. Xanthippe—you know her—was sitting beside him with their child in her arms. When she saw us, she cried out and said the sort of thing women commonly say: “Socrates, this is the last time your friends will speak with you, and you with them.” Socrates looked at Crito and said, “Crito, have someone take her home.” Some of Crito’s people led her away, crying out and beating her breast.

60b

Phaedo (continuing): Socrates sat up on the bed, bent his leg, and rubbed it with his hand. As he rubbed it, he said, “How strange this thing is, men, that people call pleasure! How wonderfully it is naturally related to what seems its opposite, pain: the two do not want to come to a person at the same time, but if someone pursues and catches one, he is almost always forced to catch the other as well—as if the two were joined at a single head—”

60c

Phaedo (continuing): “—though they are two. I think that if Aesop had noticed this, he would have made up a fable: the god wanted to reconcile them while they were fighting, but since he could not, he joined their heads together. That is why, whenever one comes to someone, the other follows afterward. It seems the same thing has happened to me: the pain in my leg, brought on by the fetter, is now followed, it seems, by pleasure.”

Cebes broke in: “By Zeus, Socrates, you did well to remind me. There is something I wanted to ask you about—

60d

Cebes (continuing): “—your poems, the ones in which you versified Aesop’s fables and composed the prelude to Apollo. Several people have already asked me about them, and very recently Evenus too. He wanted to know what you had in mind when you composed them after you came here, though you had never composed anything before. So if you care that I should have an answer ready for Evenus when he asks me again—and I know he will—tell me what I should say.”

Socrates: “Then tell him the truth, Cebes: I did not compose these poems to rival him or his poetry—I knew that would not be easy—but to test what certain dreams meant, and to clear myself in case they had often been commanding me to practice this kind of poetry.

60e

Socrates (continuing): “The dreams were something like this. Throughout my life the same dream had often visited me, appearing at different times in different forms, but always saying the same thing: ‘Socrates, make music and work at it.’ And in earlier times I took what I was doing to be the dream’s counsel to me—

61a

Socrates (continuing): “—and urging me on, as people urge runners. I thought the dream was telling me to do what I was already doing—make music—since philosophy is the greatest music and that was what I practiced. But now, after the trial had taken place and the god’s festival had kept me from dying, it seemed I ought not disobey the dream if it was perhaps repeatedly commanding me to practice this popular kind of music, but should do it. For it would be safer not to leave before I had cleared myself—”

61b

Socrates (continuing): “—by composing poems and obeying the dream. So first I composed a poem for the god whose festival was being celebrated. After that, I reflected that a poet, if he is really going to be a poet, must compose stories rather than arguments, and that I myself was not a maker of stories. So I took the fables of Aesop that I had at hand and knew, and put the first ones I came upon into verse. Tell Evenus this, Cebes, and wish him well; tell him to follow me as soon as possible, if he has any sense. I am leaving today, it seems; that is what the Athenians command.”

61c

Simmias: “What a message you are sending, Socrates, to Evenus! I have met the man many times, and from what I can tell, he will not willingly follow you at all.”

Socrates: “What? Isn’t Evenus a philosopher?”

Simmias: “I think he is.”

Socrates: “Then Evenus will be willing, and so will anyone who properly shares in this pursuit. He will not, perhaps, use force on himself, though they say that is not permitted.” As he said this, he let his legs down to the ground and sat up. From then on he continued the conversation in that position.

61d

Cebes: “How do you mean this, Socrates—that it is not permitted to use force on oneself, while the philosopher would be willing to follow someone who is dying?”

Socrates: “What, Cebes? Haven’t you and Simmias heard about such matters from Philolaus, when you were with him?”

Cebes: “Nothing clear, Socrates.”

Socrates: “Well, I too speak about them from hearsay. What I have happened to hear, I don’t begrudge telling you.”

61e

Socrates (continuing): “Perhaps it is especially fitting for someone who is about to travel there to consider and tell stories about what we think that journey will be like. What else could one do in the time until sunset?”

Cebes: “Then why do they say, Socrates, that it is not permitted to kill oneself? I have already heard from Philolaus, when he was staying with us, just what you asked me about a moment ago, and from some others too, that one should not do this. But I have never heard anything clear from anyone about the matter.”

62a

Socrates: “You must make an effort; perhaps you will hear. Still, perhaps it will seem strange to you if this one thing alone among everything is simple, and it never happens to human beings, as with other things, that there is a time when, and someone for whom, it is better to be dead than alive. And perhaps it seems strange that those for whom it is better to be dead are not permitted to do themselves a good turn, but must wait for another benefactor.”

Cebes gave a quiet laugh and said in his own dialect, “Zeus knows!”

62b

Socrates: “And so it might indeed seem irrational, though perhaps there is some reason behind it. The account told in secret teachings about this matter—that we human beings are in a kind of prison and must not release ourselves or run away from it—seems to me weighty and not easy to see through. Still, Cebes, this much seems well said to me: that the gods care for us, and that we human beings are one of the gods’ possessions. Does that seem so to you?”

Cebes: “It does,” he said.

62c

Socrates: “Then, if one of your possessions killed itself, without your having given a sign that you wanted it to die, wouldn’t you be angry with it, and, if you had some punishment available, wouldn’t you punish it?”

Cebes: “Certainly.”

Socrates: “Perhaps, then, in this way it is not irrational that one must not kill oneself before a god sends some necessity, such as the one now upon us.”

Cebes: “That seems likely, at least. But what you were saying just now—that philosophers would readily be willing to die—seems strange, Socrates, if what we said just now is reasonable: that a god cares for us and we are his possessions.”

62d

Cebes (continuing): “For it makes no sense that the wisest people should not resent leaving this service, where the gods—the best possible overseers—supervise them. Surely they do not think they will take better care of themselves once they are free. A foolish person might think that these things should be fled—”

62e

Cebes (continuing): “—from his master, and would not reason that one must not flee from what is good, but stay with it as long as possible; so he would flee without reason. A thoughtful person, surely, would always want to remain with what is better than himself. And so, Socrates, the opposite of what we said a moment ago is likely to be true: the wise should resent dying, and the foolish should welcome it.”

63a

Phaedo: When Socrates heard this, he seemed pleased by Cebes’ reasoning. He looked at us and said, “Cebes is always searching out arguments, and is never at all ready to be persuaded at once by what someone says.”

Simmias: “But Socrates, it seems to me that Cebes has a point now. Why would truly wise men want to flee from masters better than themselves and readily leave them? I think Cebes is directing his argument at you, since you take it so calmly that you are leaving us and the good rulers whom you yourself acknowledge—the gods.”

63b

Socrates: “You are right,” he said. “I think you mean that I must make my defense against these charges as if I were in court.”

Simmias: “Exactly.”

Socrates: “Come, then, let me try to make a more persuasive defense to you than I did to the judges. Simmias and Cebes, if I did not expect first to go to other gods who are wise and good, and then also to people who have died, who are better than those here, I would be wrong not to resent death.”

63c

Socrates (continuing): “But as it is, know well that I hope to join good men—and I would not insist too strongly on that. But that I will join gods who are very good masters, know well that I would insist on this, if on anything of that kind. For this reason I do not resent it in the same way, but am hopeful that there is something for the dead, and, as has long been said, that it is much better for the good than for the bad.”

63d

Simmias: “What then, Socrates? Are you going away with this thought in mind, or will you share it with us?”

Simmias (continuing): “I think this is a good we can share, and at the same time it will serve as your defense if you persuade us of what you say.”

Socrates: “I will try. But first let us see what it is that Crito here seems to have wanted to say for some time.”

Crito: “What else, Socrates, but what the man who is going to give you the poison has been telling me for a while: that you should speak as little as possible. He says people get warmer by talking, and one must not do anything like that before giving—

63e

Crito (continuing): “—the drug; otherwise, sometimes people who do such things have to drink it twice or even three times.”

Socrates: “Let him go hang,” he said. “Let him just be ready to give it twice, or even three times if necessary.”

Crito: “I rather knew that was what you would say, but he has been making trouble for me for some time.”

Socrates: “Let him be.”

64a

Socrates (continuing): “But now I want to make my defense to you, my judges: it seems to me entirely reasonable that a man who has truly spent his life in philosophy should be confident as he is about to die, and hopeful that he will gain the greatest good there once he has died. How this can be so, Simmias and Cebes, I’ll try to explain. Those who take up philosophy in the right way may well escape other people’s notice: they are practicing nothing other than dying and being dead. If that is true, it would surely be absurd to spend one’s whole life eagerly practicing this, and then to be distressed when the very thing one has long practiced and pursued finally arrives.”

Simmias laughed. “By Zeus, Socrates—”

64b

Simmias (continuing): “You’ve made me laugh, though I wasn’t much in a laughing mood just now. I think most people who heard you would say that was very well put against philosophers—and our people here would especially agree—that philosophers really are as good as dead, and they know perfectly well that they deserve it.”

Socrates: “And they would be telling the truth, Simmias, except that they don’t know this about themselves. They don’t know in what way true philosophers are practicing death, in what way they deserve death, or what kind of death it is.”

64c

Socrates (continuing): “Let’s talk this over among ourselves, and leave those people aside. Do we think death is something?”

Simmias: “Certainly,” he replied.

Socrates: “Is it anything other than the soul’s release from the body? And is being dead this: the body, once released from the soul, becoming by itself on its own; and the soul, once released from the body, being by itself on its own? Or is death anything other than this?”

Simmias: “No, it is just that.”

Socrates: “Then see, my good friend, whether you agree with me about what follows. I think that from this we will better understand what we are investigating.”

64d

Socrates (continuing): “Does it seem to you fitting for a philosophical man to be seriously concerned with the so-called pleasures of eating and drinking?”

Simmias: “Not at all, Socrates,” said Simmias.

Socrates: “What about the pleasures of sex?”

Simmias: “Not at all.”

Socrates: “And what about the other kinds of care for the body? Does a man of this sort seem to you to value them? For example, the acquisition of fine clothes and shoes, and the other adornments of the body: do you think he honors these or looks down on them—”

64e

Socrates (continuing): “—except insofar as great necessity requires him to take part in them?”

Simmias: “A true philosopher, I think, looks down on them.”

Socrates: “So, taken as a whole, do you think this man’s concern is not with the body but, as far as he can, with standing apart from it and turning toward the soul?”

Simmias: “I do.”

65a

Socrates: “Then first of all, in such matters, isn’t it clear that a philosopher releases his soul from its partnership with the body more than other people do?”

Simmias: “It seems so.”

Socrates: “And most people think, Simmias, that a person who takes no pleasure in such things and has no share in them is hardly worth living; that anyone who cares nothing for the pleasures that come through the body is close to being dead.”

Simmias: “That is certainly true.”

Socrates: “Now, what about the acquisition of wisdom itself? Is the body a hindrance or not, if someone, in the course of inquiry—”

65b

Socrates (continuing): “—as a partner in the search? I mean something like this: do sight and hearing give human beings any truth? Or is it as the poets keep telling us, that we neither hear nor see anything accurately? And if these bodily senses are neither accurate nor clear, the others can hardly be so, since they are all, I suppose, inferior to these. Don’t you think so?”

Simmias: “Entirely.”

Socrates (continuing): “When, then, does the soul touch the truth? For whenever it tries to examine something with the body, it is clear that the body deceives it.”

65c

Simmias: “You’re right.”

Socrates: “Then isn’t it in reasoning, if anywhere, that something of what is can become clear to it?”

Simmias: “Yes.”

Socrates: “And it reasons best, I suppose, when none of these things troubles it—neither hearing nor sight, neither pain nor any pleasure—but when it becomes as much as possible by itself, letting the body go, and, as far as it can, without sharing in or being in contact with the body, reaches out toward what is.”

Simmias: “That’s so.”

Socrates: “So here too the philosopher’s soul most of all—”

65d

Socrates (continuing): “—looks down on the body and flees from it, seeking to become by itself?”

Simmias: “It seems so.”

Socrates (continuing): “And what about things like these, Simmias? Do we say that there is such a thing as the Just itself, or nothing of the sort?”

Simmias: “By Zeus, we certainly do.”

Socrates: “And likewise the Beautiful and the Good?”

Simmias: “Of course.”

Socrates: “Have you ever seen any of these with your eyes?”

Simmias: “Not at all,” he replied.

Socrates: “Or have you grasped them by any other bodily sense? I mean all such things: greatness, health, strength, and, in a word, the being of everything—what each thing—”

65e

Socrates (continuing): “—in fact is. Is its truest reality seen through the body? Or is it this way: whoever most carefully prepares himself to think through each thing he is examining comes closest to knowing it?”

Simmias: “Certainly.”

Socrates (continuing): “Then who would do this most purely? The one who approaches each thing as much as possible by thought alone, bringing no sight into his thinking, and drawing in no other—”

66a

Socrates (continuing): “—sense alongside his reasoning, but using pure thought by itself to try to hunt down each pure thing that exists on its own—freeing himself, as far as possible, from eyes and ears and, so to speak, the whole body, since the body disturbs the soul and does not let it acquire truth and wisdom whenever it shares in the inquiry? Isn’t this person, Simmias, if anyone is, the one who will reach what is?”

Simmias: “What you say is wonderfully true, Socrates.”

66b

Socrates: “Then from all this, a thought of this kind must arise in genuine philosophers, and lead them to say to one another something like this: ‘It seems there is a sort of path that can guide us in our reasoning: as long as we have the body and our soul is mixed up with such an evil, we will never adequately attain what we desire. And what we desire, we say, is the truth. The body causes us countless distractions because it must be fed.’”

66c

Socrates (continuing): “‘And if illnesses of any kind come upon us, they hinder our hunt for what is. The body fills us with loves, desires, fears, all sorts of images, and a great deal of nonsense, so that, as the saying goes, it never lets us think clearly about anything. For wars, factions, and battles come from nothing but the body and its desires. All wars arise because of the acquisition of wealth, and wealth—’”

66d

Socrates (continuing): “‘—we are forced to acquire because of the body, serving its needs. And so, for all these reasons, we have no time for philosophy. Worst of all, even if we get some free time from it and turn to examine something, in the course of our investigations it barges in everywhere, causing turmoil and confusion and overwhelming us, so that we cannot see the truth because of it. In fact, we have been shown that if we are ever to know anything purely—’”

66e

Socrates (continuing): “‘—we must be rid of the body and examine the things themselves with the soul by itself. Then, it seems, we will have what we desire and claim to love—wisdom—when we die, as the argument indicates, but not while we live. For if it is impossible to know anything purely while we are in the body, one of two things must be true: either knowledge cannot be acquired anywhere, or only after death; for then—’”

67a

Socrates (continuing): “‘—the soul will be by itself, apart from the body, but not before. While we live, it seems, we will be closest to knowing if we associate with the body as little as possible and share in it only when necessity requires; if we do not fill ourselves with its nature but keep ourselves clear of it until the god himself releases us. And so, purified as we leave the body’s foolishness, we will, as is likely, be with others like ourselves and know through ourselves—’”

67b

Socrates (continuing): “‘—everything that is pure. This perhaps is the truth: the impure is not permitted to touch the pure.’”

Socrates (continuing): “I think, Simmias, that all genuine lovers of learning must say and believe these things to one another. Don’t you think so?”

Simmias: “Absolutely, Socrates.”

67c

Socrates: “Then, my friend, if this is true, there is good reason to hope that when I arrive where I am going, I will there, more than anywhere else, attain what we have devoted so much effort to in our past life. So this journey—”

Socrates (continuing): “—now imposed on me is undertaken with good hope. The same goes for any other man who believes his mind has been prepared and, as it were, purified.”

Simmias: “Certainly.”

Socrates (continuing): “And isn’t purification just what we have been talking about for some time: separating the soul as much as possible from the body and accustoming it to gather itself together, drawing it in from every part of the body, and to live, as far as it can, both in the present and in the—”

67d

Socrates (continuing): “—time to come, alone by itself, released from the body as from chains?”

Simmias: “Certainly.”

Socrates: “And this is what we call death: the soul’s release and separation from the body?”

Simmias: “Exactly,” he said.

Socrates: “And those who practice philosophy rightly are always, and they alone, most eager to release the soul; that is the very practice of philosophers, the soul’s release and separation from the body. Isn’t that so?”

Simmias: “It seems so.”

Socrates (continuing): “Then, as I said at the beginning, wouldn’t it be ridiculous for a man—”

67e

Socrates: “—who prepares himself throughout life to be as close as possible to being dead, and then to be distressed when death comes to him?”

Simmias: “Ridiculous indeed. How could it be otherwise?”

Socrates (continuing): “So, Simmias, those who practice philosophy rightly are in truth practicing death, and death is least frightening to them of all people. Consider this: if they are at odds in every way with the body and desire to have the soul by itself, wouldn’t it be deeply irrational, when this happens, for them to be afraid and distressed and not—”

68a

Socrates (continuing): “—gladly go there, where, on arrival, they hope to gain what they have loved all their lives—and what they loved was wisdom—and be rid of what they were at odds with? Many people, when the boys they loved, their wives, or their sons had died, had willingly wanted to go to Hades, led by the hope of seeing and being with those they longed for. Should someone who truly loves wisdom, and has firmly grasped this same hope, be distressed as he dies and unwilling to go there, when he expects to meet wisdom nowhere else in a worthy way but—”

68b

Socrates (continuing): “—in Hades? We should think so, my friend, if he is truly a philosopher. For he will be firmly convinced that nowhere else can he meet wisdom in its purity but there. If this is so, wouldn’t it be deeply irrational for such a man to fear death?”

Simmias said, “By Zeus, it certainly would.”

Socrates (continuing): “Then when you see a man distressed at the prospect of dying, isn’t that sufficient evidence that he was not—”

68c

Socrates (continuing): “—a philosopher but a lover of the body? And that same man is surely also a lover of money and a lover of honor—or perhaps one of these, or both.”

Simmias: “It is just as you say.”

Socrates (continuing): “And doesn’t what is called courage especially belong to those who are disposed in this way, Simmias?”

Simmias: “Certainly.”

Socrates: “And also self-control, which most people call self-control: not being carried away by desires, but holding them in check and behaving with discipline. Doesn’t this belong only to those who most disregard the body and live in philosophy?”

68d

Simmias: “It must.”

Socrates: “For if you consider the courage and self-control of other people, they will seem strange.”

Simmias: “How so, Socrates?”

Socrates: “You know that all other people regard death as one of the great evils?”

Simmias: “Very much so.”

Socrates: “And when the brave among them face death, don’t they face it out of fear of greater evils?”

Simmias: “They do.”

Socrates: “So everyone except philosophers is brave through fear and dread. Yet it is irrational for someone to be brave through fear and cowardice.”

68e

Simmias: “Entirely.”

Cebes: Yes, indeed.

79d

Socrates: But when it examines things by itself, it goes there, to what is pure and always exists, immortal and unchanging. Since it is akin to that, it is always together with it whenever it is by itself and is able to be so. Then it stops wandering and remains, in relation to those things, always in the same state, because it is in contact with things of that sort. And this condition of the soul is called wisdom, is it not?

Cebes: You speak entirely well and truly, Socrates.

Socrates: Which kind, then, do you think the soul is more like and more akin to, on the basis of what we said before and what we are saying now?

79e

Cebes: Anyone, I think, Socrates—even the slowest learner—would agree, from this method of inquiry, that in every way the soul is more like what always remains the same than what does not.

Socrates: And the body?

Cebes: It is like the other kind.

80a

Socrates: Consider this point too: when soul and body are together, nature assigns to the one the task of serving and being ruled, and to the other that of ruling and mastering. Which, then, seems to you more like the divine, and which like the mortal? Or does it not seem to you that the divine is naturally suited to rule and lead, while the mortal is suited to be ruled and serve?

Cebes: It does.

Socrates: Which, then, is the soul like?

Cebes: It is clear, Socrates: the soul is like the divine, and the body like the mortal.

Socrates: Consider, Cebes, whether all that we have said leads to this conclusion: that the soul is most like what is divine, immortal, intelligible, uniform, indissoluble, and always in the same state, while the body is most like what is human, mortal, many-formed, unintelligible, dissoluble, and never in the same state. Have we anything else to say, dear Cebes, to show that this is not so?

Cebes: No.

80b

Socrates: Then, if these things are so, is it not fitting for the body to be dissolved quickly, while the soul is wholly indissoluble, or nearly so?

Cebes: Of course.

80c

Socrates: You realize, then, that when a human being dies, the visible part of him—his body, which lies in a visible place and is what we call a corpse, and which is naturally liable to be dissolved, scattered, and dispersed—does not immediately suffer any of this. Rather, it remains for a fairly long time, especially if someone dies with his body in good condition and at a favorable season. The body may shrink and be embalmed, as the bodies embalmed in Egypt are, and almost the whole of it remains for an astonishingly long time—

80d

Socrates (continuing): —and some parts of the body, even when it decays, such as bones, sinews, and all such things, are, so to speak, nearly immortal. Are they not?

Cebes: Yes.

Socrates: But the soul, the invisible part, goes away to another place of the same kind, noble, pure, and invisible: to Hades in truth, to the good and wise god, where—if god is willing—my soul too must soon go. Is a soul of this sort, with this nature, when it is released from the body, immediately scattered by the winds and destroyed, as most people say?

80e

Socrates (continuing): Far from it, dear Cebes and Simmias. It is much more like this: if it is released in a pure state, dragging nothing bodily along with it, since during life it willingly shared nothing with the body, but fled from it and gathered itself into itself, having continually practiced this—for this is nothing other than practicing philosophy rightly, and practicing how to be dead with ease; or is that not the practice of death?

81a

Cebes: It certainly is.

Socrates: Then, being in this condition, it goes away to what is like itself, the invisible, divine, immortal, and wise. When it arrives there, it can be happy, freed from wandering, foolishness, fears, savage desires, and the other human evils; and, as is said of the initiated, it truly spends the rest of time with the gods. Shall we say this, Cebes, or something else?

Cebes: This, by Zeus.

81b

Socrates: But if, I suppose, it is released from the body polluted and impure, because it was always with the body, serving and loving it, bewitched by it through desires and pleasures, until it thinks nothing else is real except what is bodily—what one can touch, see, drink, eat, and use for sex—while what is dim to the eyes and invisible, but intelligible and attainable by philosophy, it has become accustomed to hate, fear, and flee—do you think that such a soul will depart by itself, pure and unmingled?

81c

Cebes: Not at all.

Socrates: Rather, I suppose, it will be penetrated by what is bodily, which its companionship and constant association with the body have made natural to it through their continual presence and its long practice.

Cebes: Certainly.

Socrates: And we must think, my friend, that this bodily element is heavy, weighty, earthy, and visible. A soul that has it is weighed down and dragged back to the visible region, for fear of the invisible and of Hades. It is said to linger around tombs and graves, where shadowy apparitions of souls have sometimes been seen. Such apparitions are images produced by souls of this sort: souls not released purely, but still sharing in the visible; that is why they are seen.

81d

Cebes: That is plausible, Socrates.

Socrates: It is plausible, Cebes. And these are not the souls of good people, but of bad people, which are compelled to wander around such places, paying the penalty for their former bad upbringing. They wander until, through desire for the bodily element that accompanies them, they are bound again into a body.

81e

Socrates (continuing): And, as one would expect, they are bound into characters of the sort they happened to have practiced in life.

Cebes: What sorts do you mean, Socrates?

Socrates: For example, those who practiced gluttony, violence, and drunkenness, and took no care to avoid them, are likely to enter the kinds of asses and other such beasts—

82a

Socrates (continuing): —is that not likely?

Cebes: Very likely, as you say.

Socrates: And those who chose injustice, tyranny, and robbery above all are likely to enter the kinds of wolves, hawks, and kites. Or where else should we say such souls go?

Cebes: They go into those kinds of creatures, certainly.

Socrates: Is it not clear, then, where all the others go, each in accordance with the likeness of what they practiced?

Cebes: Clear, of course. How could it not be?

82b

Socrates: So the happiest among these, and those who go to the best place, are those who have practiced the popular and civic virtue they call self-control and justice, which comes from habit and practice, without philosophy or understanding.

Cebes: How are they the happiest?

Socrates: Because it is likely that they will again arrive in some gentle and civic kind, perhaps bees, wasps, or ants—or even return to the same human kind, becoming decent men from them.

Cebes: Likely.

82c

Socrates: But it is not permitted for one who has not practiced philosophy and has not departed wholly pure to reach the company of the gods; only the lover of learning may do so. For these reasons, my friends Simmias and Cebes, those who are rightly devoted to learning abstain from all bodily desires, endure them, and do not surrender themselves to them—not because they fear the ruin of their household or poverty, as most people do, those who love money; nor do they abstain because they fear disgrace and disrepute from wickedness, as those who love power and honor do.

Cebes: That would not be fitting, Socrates.

82d

Socrates: No, by Zeus, it would not. Therefore, Cebes, those who care for their own soul and do not live by shaping themselves around the body bid farewell to all such people. They do not walk the same road as those who do not know where they are going; instead, believing they must do nothing contrary to philosophy, to its release and purification, they follow where philosophy leads.

Cebes: How so, Socrates?

Socrates: I will tell you. Those who love learning know—

82e

Socrates (continuing): —that philosophy takes their soul in a state of utter bondage to the body, stuck fast to it; forced, as if through a prison, to examine what is by means of the body rather than by itself; and rolling in complete ignorance. Philosophy sees that the prison is terrible and that desire is what holds it there, so that the prisoner himself is the chief accomplice in his own imprisonment.

83a

Socrates (continuing): That, as I say, is what those who love learning know: philosophy gently consoles the soul in that condition and tries to release it, showing that the examination made through the eyes is full of deception, and so is the examination through the ears and the other senses. It persuades the soul to withdraw from the senses except when their use is necessary, and urges it to collect and gather itself into itself—

83b

Socrates (continuing): —and to trust no one else, but itself alone, whenever, by itself, it understands something that is itself by itself among the things that are. Whatever it examines through other means, in other things, as one thing existing in another, it must consider nothing true. What is of that sort is perceptible and visible; what the soul itself sees is intelligible and invisible. The soul of the true philosopher, believing it must not oppose this release, therefore abstains, as far as it can, from pleasures, desires, and pains. [The source marks “and fears” as deleted here.]

83c

Socrates (continuing): It reasons that whenever someone feels intense pleasure, fear, pain, or desire, he suffers no such great harm from them as one might think—for example, by falling ill or spending money because of his desires. Rather, he suffers the greatest and most extreme of evils, and does not reckon it as such.

Cebes: What is that, Socrates?

Socrates: Every human soul is forced, at the very moment it feels intense pleasure or pain about something, to suppose that whatever it is most affected by is clearest and truest, though it is not so. These are above all visible things, are they not?

Cebes: Very much so.

83d

Socrates: Is it not in this condition above all that the soul is bound by the body?

Cebes: How so?

Socrates: Because each pleasure and pain, like a nail, pins the soul to the body, fastens it there, and makes it bodily in character, so that it believes true whatever the body says is true. From sharing the body's beliefs and taking pleasure in the same things, it is compelled, I think, to acquire the same ways and habits, and to become the sort of thing that can never arrive in Hades in a pure state. Instead it leaves full of body, and so soon falls again into another body and grows into it as if sown; as a result, it has no share in the company of the divine, pure, and uniform.

83e

Cebes: You speak most truly, Socrates.

Socrates: For these reasons, Cebes, those who are rightly devoted to learning are disciplined and courageous—not for the reasons the many give. Or do you think otherwise?

84a

Cebes: No, I do not.

Socrates: No indeed. A philosopher's soul would reason in this way. It would not think that while philosophy is trying to release it, it should then hand itself over again to pleasures and pains, bind itself back into them, and undertake a task without end—like Penelope weaving her web backward. Instead, it brings calm to these things, follows reason, and is always with it, contemplating what is true, divine, and not a matter of opinion—

84b

Socrates (continuing): —and being nourished by that. It thinks it ought to live this way as long as it lives; and when it dies, it expects to reach what is akin to it and of the same sort, and to be freed from human evils. From such nourishment there is no reason for it to fear, Simmias and Cebes, that when it is released from the body it might be torn apart, blown away by the winds, scattered, and cease to exist anywhere.

84c

After Socrates had said this, there was a long silence. Socrates himself, as it seemed, was still absorbed in the argument he had just given, and so were most of us. But Cebes and Simmias spoke quietly to one another. Socrates noticed and asked, “What is it? Do you think what has been said is inadequate? It certainly leaves many doubts and objections, if someone is to examine it thoroughly. If you are considering something else, I have nothing to say; but if you are puzzled about any of these points, do not hesitate to speak and go through them yourselves, if you think they could be stated better. And take me along too, if you think we can make more progress together.”

84d

Simmias: Socrates, I will tell you the truth. For some time each of us has been troubled and has been urging the other to ask, because we want to hear, but hesitate to cause you trouble in case it is unpleasant to you in your present misfortune.

Socrates heard him, laughed softly, and said, “Dear me, Simmias, I would have a hard time persuading other people that I do not regard my present lot as a misfortune, when I cannot even persuade you. You are afraid that I am in a more difficult state of mind now than in my earlier life; and it seems you think me a poorer prophet than the swans—

84e

Socrates (continuing): —which, when they realize they must die, sing, and even in—

85a

Socrates (continuing): “—the time before as well, but then they sing the most and most beautifully, rejoicing that they are about to go to the god whose servants they are.”

Socrates (continuing): “But people, because of their own fear of death, tell lies about the swans too. They say that the swans sing their dirge for death, lamenting it in grief. They do not consider that no bird sings when it is hungry or cold, or suffers any other pain—not even the nightingale, the swallow, or the hoopoe, which they say sing dirges in grief. It seems to me that neither these birds nor the swans sing because they are grieving. Rather, because they belong to Apollo, I think, they are prophetic, and, foreknowing the good things in Hades, they sing and take special delight on that day, more than at any earlier time. I too believe that I am a fellow-servant of the swans and consecrated to the same god, and that I have no worse gift of prophecy from my master than they do; and I am no more downcast than they are at leaving this life. So, for this reason, you should speak and ask whatever you wish, as long as the Eleven allow it.”

85b

Simmias: “You speak well,” he said, “and I too will tell you what is troubling me, and this man here will tell you what he does not accept. For I think, Socrates, that perhaps—as you do—knowing clearly about such matters in this life is either impossible or extremely difficult.”

85c

Simmias (continuing): “Yet not to test in every way what is said about them, and not to give up before someone has examined it from every side and is worn out, would be the mark of a very weak man. One must accomplish one of these things about them: either learn how they stand, or find it out, or, if that is impossible, take the best and hardest-to-refute of human accounts and, riding on it as on a raft, sail through life at risk—unless one can make the journey more safely and with less danger on a firmer vessel, some divine account.”

85d

Simmias (continuing): “And now, since you yourself put it this way, I will not be ashamed to ask, nor later blame myself for not saying now what I think. For when I examine what has been said, both by myself and with this man here, it does not seem to me to have been stated quite adequately.”

85e

Socrates: “Perhaps, my friend, it seems true to you,” he said, “but tell me in what way it is not adequate.”

86a

Simmias: “In this way,” he said. “Someone might make the same argument about a harmony, a lyre, and its strings. The harmony is invisible, incorporeal, altogether beautiful, and divine, in the tuned lyre; but the lyre itself and its strings are bodies, bodily in kind, composite, earthy, and akin to what is mortal. Suppose someone broke the lyre, or cut and snapped its strings, and then insisted on the same argument you do: that the harmony must still exist and have not perished. For there would be no way that the lyre, with its strings broken, and the strings, which are mortal in kind, could still exist, while the harmony, which is akin to the divine and immortal and of the same kind, perished first—”

86b

Simmias (continuing): “—before what is mortal. Rather, that person would say the harmony itself must still exist somewhere, and the wood and strings would rot before anything happened to it. Indeed, Socrates, I think you yourself have considered that this is more or less how we take the soul to be: as the tension of our body, held together by hot and cold, dry and wet, and other such things; our soul is a kind of blending and harmony of these elements when they are well and proportionately blended with one another.”

86c

Simmias (continuing): “If, then, the soul happens to be a kind of harmony, it is clear that when our body is relaxed without measure or overstretched by diseases and other evils, the soul must at once perish, though it is most divine—just like the other harmonies, whether in sounds or in all the works of craftsmen. The remains of each body, meanwhile, last for a long time—”

86d

Simmias (continuing): “—until they are burned or rot away. So consider what we shall say to this argument, if someone insists that the soul, being a blending of the elements in the body, is the first to perish in what is called death.”

Phaedo: Socrates looked keenly at us, as he often did, and smiled. “Simmias certainly has a fair point,” he said. “So if any of you is better supplied than I am, why has he not answered? He seems to have got a good grip on the argument. Still, I think we should hear first what Cebes here objects to, so that we have time to consider what we shall say. Then, after listening, we can either agree with them, if they seem to have a point, or else defend the argument.”

86e

Socrates: “Come now,” he said, “Cebes, tell us what it was that is troubling you and making you doubt.”

87a

Cebes: “I will tell you. It seems to me that the argument is still in the same position, and, as we said earlier, faces the same objection. I do not deny that our soul existed even before it entered this form; that has been demonstrated very elegantly and, if it is not tiresome to say so, quite adequately.”

87b

Cebes (continuing): “But I do not think it has been shown that the soul exists somewhere after we have died. I do not agree with Simmias’ objection that the soul is not stronger and longer-lived than the body; in my view, it is far superior to the body in all these respects. So, the argument might say, why do you still doubt, since you see that what is weaker continues to exist after a human being has died? Don’t you think it necessary that what lasts longer should also survive that span of time? Consider this next point, and see whether I am saying anything. It seems I too, like Simmias, need an image.”

87c

Cebes (continuing): “What is being said seems to me like someone making this argument about an old weaver who has died: ‘The man has not perished; he is still somewhere safe.’ As proof, he might produce the cloak the man wore and wove himself, still intact and not perished. If someone doubted him, he might ask whether the human race or a cloak in use and being worn lasts longer. When the other answered that the human race lasts far longer, he might think he had proved that the man was certainly safe, since what lasts for less time had not perished.”

87d

Cebes (continuing): “But that is not how it is, I think, Simmias. Look at what I mean. Anyone would think that the man who said this was foolish: this weaver wore out and wove many such cloaks, and he perished after many of them, though before the last one. Yet this does not make a human being any more inferior or weaker than a cloak. I think the same image would be accepted for the soul in relation to the body. Anyone speaking in this way about them would seem to me to speak reasonably: the soul lasts a long time, while the body is weaker and lasts a shorter time.”

87e

Cebes (continuing): “But the person might say that each soul wears out many bodies, especially if it lives for many years. For even if the body is continually flowing away and being destroyed while the person is still alive, the soul would continually weave anew what has been worn away—”

88a

Cebes (continuing): “—it would necessarily be the case that, whenever the soul perished, it would have its last weaving on, and perish before only that. But once the soul had perished, the body would then reveal the nature of its weakness and quickly rot away and disappear. So we are not yet justified in trusting this argument and confidently believing that our soul still exists somewhere after we die.”

88b

Cebes (continuing): “For even if someone granted the speaker still more than you say, and conceded not only that our souls existed before we came to be, but that there is nothing to prevent some of them from continuing to exist and to be after we die, and from being born and dying again many times—since the soul is naturally so strong that it can endure being born again and again—still, if he granted all this, he might refuse to concede one thing: that the soul does not labor through all these births and, at last, perish completely in one of its deaths. He might say that no one knows this particular death, this dissolution of the body that brings destruction to the soul—”

88c

Cebes (continuing): “—for it is impossible for any of us to perceive it. If this is so, no one is entitled to face death confidently without being foolish, unless he can prove that the soul is altogether immortal and imperishable. Otherwise, anyone about to die must always fear for his own soul, lest it be utterly destroyed in its present separation from the body.”

Phaedo: We all felt unpleasantly affected after hearing the two men speak. As we said to one another later, they seemed to have upset us badly again, though we had been thoroughly convinced by the earlier argument, and to have thrown us back into distrust—not only toward the arguments already given, but also toward those still to come. We began to fear that we were incompetent judges, or that the matters themselves were beyond belief.

88d

Echecrates: “By the gods, Phaedo, I understand you. Even as I listened to you just now, I found myself thinking, ‘What argument can we trust now? The argument Socrates gave was so convincing, and yet it has now fallen into doubt.’ This argument that the soul is a kind of harmony grips me remarkably, both now and always. Hearing it has reminded me that I had already held this view myself. So I badly need another argument, as if starting over, to convince me that the soul does not die along with the dying person. Tell me, by Zeus, how did Socrates take up the argument?”

88e

Echecrates (continuing): “Did he show that he was troubled too, as you say you were, or did he calmly come to the argument’s aid? And did he defend it adequately, or not enough? Tell us the whole story as precisely as you can.”

89a

Phaedo: “Echecrates, though I have often admired Socrates, I was never more filled with admiration than when I was there on that occasion. Perhaps there is nothing strange in his having an answer. What I admired most was first how pleasantly, kindly, and approvingly he received the young men’s argument; then how keenly he noticed the effect their arguments had had on us; and then how well he healed us, as if we had been fleeing and defeated, calling us back and urging us to follow the argument and examine it together.”

89b

Echecrates: “How so?”

Phaedo: “I’ll tell you. I happened to be sitting to his right beside the bed, on a low stool, while he sat much higher than I did. He stroked my head and pressed down the hair on the back of my neck—he often played with my hair when he had the chance. ‘Perhaps tomorrow, Phaedo,’ he said, ‘you will have those lovely locks of yours cut off.’”

89c

Phaedo (continuing): “‘That seems likely, Socrates,’ I said.

“‘Not if you take my advice.’

“‘Why not?’

“‘Today, I will cut mine, and you will cut yours too, if our argument reaches its end and we cannot bring it back to life.’

“‘If I were you and the argument escaped me, I would make an oath like the Argives: not to grow my hair again before I had won a rematch against Simmias’ and Cebes’ argument.’

“‘But,’ I said, ‘not even Heracles, they say, can fight two at once.’”

89d

Phaedo (continuing): “‘Then call me too, as your Iolaus, while there is still daylight.’

“‘I call you, then—not as Heracles calling Iolaus, but as Iolaus calling Heracles.’

“‘It will make no difference,’ he said. ‘But first let us take care not to suffer a certain thing.’

“‘What thing?’ I asked.

“‘That we do not become haters of argument, as people become haters of human beings. No greater evil can happen to someone than to hate arguments. Hating argument and hating people arise in the same way. Hatred of people comes from trusting someone too completely, without skill, and thinking that person entirely truthful, sound, and trustworthy; then, a little later, finding that the person is wicked and untrustworthy—and then the same with someone else. When someone has suffered this often, especially at the hands of those he thought—’”

89e

Socrates (continuing, as reported by Phaedo): “‘—closest and dearest, he ends up repeatedly clashing with people, hating them all, and believing that no one is sound in any respect. Have you ever noticed this happening?’

“‘I certainly have,’ I said.

“‘Isn’t that shameful,’ he said, ‘and isn’t it clear that such a person tried to deal with people without skill in human affairs?’”

90a

Phaedo (continuing): “‘If he had dealt with people skillfully, he would have judged things as they are: that the very good and the very bad are both quite few, while those in between are numerous.’

“‘What do you mean?’ I asked.

“‘Just as with things that are extremely small or large. Do you think it is rare to find a very tall or very short human being, dog, or anything else? Or one that is fast or slow, ugly or beautiful, white or black? Have you not noticed that in all such cases the extremes are rare and few, while what lies between is plentiful and numerous?’

“‘Certainly,’ I said.

“‘And do you think, if a contest in wickedness were announced, that very few would also appear first in that?’”

90b

Phaedo (continuing): “‘That is likely,’ I said.

“‘It is likely,’ he said. ‘But in that respect arguments are not like people. I followed the lead you just gave. They are like people in this respect: when someone trusts an argument as true without skill in arguments, and then a little later it seems false to him—sometimes it is, sometimes it is not—and the same happens with one argument after another—’”

90c

Socrates (continuing, as reported by Phaedo): “‘—you know that, especially those who spend their time on arguments for and against end up thinking they have become the wisest people and alone have understood that neither in things nor in arguments is there anything sound or stable; rather, everything that exists turns up and down, like things in the Euripus, and never remains in one place for any time.’

“‘You speak very truly,’ I said.

“‘Wouldn’t it be a pitiful state,’ he said, ‘if there is some true, stable argument that can be understood, but then, because a person encounters arguments of this sort—the very same ones seeming sometimes true and sometimes not—he blames neither himself nor his own lack of skill—’”

90d

Socrates (continuing): “‘—and ends up, in his pain, gladly shifting the blame away from himself onto the arguments? Then he spends the rest of his life hating and reviling them, and is deprived of truth and knowledge of what is.’

“‘By Zeus,’ I said, ‘it certainly would be pitiful.’

“‘First, then,’ he said, ‘let us take care of this, and not let it enter our souls that there may be nothing sound in arguments—’”

90e

Socrates (continuing): “‘—but much rather that we ourselves are not yet sound, and that we must be courageous and eager to become sound: you and the others for the sake of your whole life to come, and I for the sake of death itself. For at this moment, I am in danger of approaching this very matter not philosophically, but like those who are utterly uneducated, in a spirit of winning at all costs.’”

91a

Phaedo: We agreed that this was so.

Socrates (continuing): “For those people, when they dispute about something, do not care how things stand about the subject they are discussing. They care only that what they themselves have laid down should seem so to those present. I think that at this moment I will differ from them only in this: I will not be eager to make what I say seem true to those present, except as a secondary matter, but rather to make it seem as true as possible to myself—”

91b

Socrates (continuing): “—for I am calculating, my dear friend—see how self-interestedly—that if what I say happens to be true, it is well to be persuaded; but if there is nothing for a person who has died, at least for this time, the time just before death, I will be less unpleasant to those present by not lamenting. And my foolishness will not last alongside me—for that would be bad—but will perish a little later.

“So, Simmias and Cebes, I come prepared in this way to the argument. You, if you take my advice—”

91c

Socrates (continuing): “—will care little for Socrates and much more for the truth. If you think I say something true, agree with me; if not, resist me with every argument. Take care that, in my eagerness, I do not deceive both myself and you, and then leave like a bee, leaving its sting behind.

“Come, we must proceed. First remind me what you were saying, in case I do not seem to remember. As I understand it, Simmias doubts and fears that the soul, though more divine and beautiful than the body, may perish first because it is a kind of harmony—”

91d

Socrates (continuing): “—while Cebes, I think, granted me that the soul lasts longer than the body, but said it was unclear to everyone whether the soul, after wearing out many bodies many times, may itself now perish when it leaves the last body; and that this very thing might be death—the soul’s destruction—since the body never stops perishing. Is there anything else, Simmias and Cebes, that we must examine?”

91e

Phaedo: They both agreed that these were the points.

Socrates: “Then do you reject all the arguments that came before, or some and not others?”

Simmias and Cebes: “Some, but not others.”

Socrates (continuing): “Then what do you say about that argument in which you were convinced—”

92a

Socrates (continuing, as reported by Phaedo): “—that the soul existed before it was bound into the body?”

Cebes: “I was wonderfully persuaded by him then, and I still hold to that view as firmly as to any argument.”

Simmias: “I feel the same way, and I would be very surprised if I ever came to think otherwise about it.”

Socrates: “But you must think otherwise, my Theban friend, if this belief remains with you: that a harmony is a composite thing, and the soul is a kind of harmony made up from the tensions in the body. Surely you would not accept your own claim that a harmony was already composed before the things from which it had to be composed existed. Would you?”

92b

Simmias: “Not at all, Socrates.”

Socrates: “Then do you see what follows from what you are saying, when you say that the soul existed before it arrived in the form and body of a human being, yet that it is composed from things that did not yet exist? For what you are comparing it to is nothing like that. The lyre, its strings, and the notes are there first, still untuned; the harmony is put together last of all, and is the first thing to perish.”

92c

Socrates (continuing): “How, then, can this account agree with the other?”

Simmias: “It cannot,” he said.

Socrates: “And yet, if any account ought to agree with another, it is surely the account of harmony.”

Simmias: “It ought to.”

Socrates: “This one does not agree with it. So decide which account you choose: that learning is recollection, or that the soul is a harmony.”

Simmias: “I choose the former by far, Socrates. This latter account came to me without proof, through a certain plausibility and elegance, which is why most people accept it. I know that arguments which establish their proofs through plausibility are impostors, and, unless one is on one’s guard, they deceive very thoroughly—in geometry and in all other subjects.”

92d

Simmias (continuing): “But the account of recollection and learning was stated on the basis of a hypothesis worth accepting. For we said that our soul existed even before it arrived in a body, just as its being has the name ‘what is.’”

92e

Simmias (continuing): “I have accepted that account, as far as I can persuade myself, sufficiently and correctly. So it seems I must not accept, from myself or anyone else, the claim that the soul is a harmony.”

Socrates: “And what about this, Simmias? Does it belong to a harmony, or to any other composite, to be in any way different from the way the things from which it is composed are?”

93a

Simmias: “Not at all.”

Socrates: “Nor, I suppose, to do or undergo anything other than what those things do or undergo?”

Simmias: “No.”

Socrates: “So a harmony does not lead the things from which it is composed; it follows them.”

Simmias: “I agree.”

Socrates: “Then a harmony is far from being able to move, make a sound, or otherwise oppose its own parts.”

Simmias: “Far indeed.”

Socrates: “And isn’t each harmony naturally a harmony in the way it has been tuned?”

Simmias: “I don’t understand.”

93b

Socrates: “If it is more fully and more extensively tuned—if that can happen—it would be more of a harmony and a greater harmony; and if less fully and less extensively, it would be a lesser and smaller harmony?”

Simmias: “Certainly.”

Socrates: “Now, does anything like this happen to one soul in comparison with another, so that one soul is even by the smallest degree more or less a soul than another?”

Simmias: “Not in the least.”

Socrates: “Come, then, by Zeus: is it said that one soul has intelligence, virtue, and goodness, while another has foolishness and wickedness and is bad? And is this truly said?”

Simmias: “Truly.”

93c

Socrates: “Then what will those who say the soul is a harmony say these things are in souls—virtue and vice? Another harmony and a lack of harmony? Will they say that the good soul is tuned and contains within itself another harmony, while the bad soul is itself untuned and contains no other harmony?”

Simmias: “I cannot say. But it is clear that someone who assumes that will say something of the sort.”

93d

Socrates: “But it was agreed earlier that one soul is no more or less a soul than another. And that agreement is the same as saying that one harmony is no more or less a harmony than another. Is that right?”

Simmias: “Certainly.”

Socrates: “And a harmony that is no more or less a harmony is neither more nor less tuned. Is that so?”

Simmias: “Yes.”

Socrates: “And what is neither more nor less tuned—does it share in harmony to a greater or lesser degree, or equally?”

Simmias: “Equally.”

Socrates: “Then, since one soul is no more or less a soul than another, neither is one more or less tuned than another.”

93e

Simmias: “That follows.”

Socrates: “And in that condition, it would share no more in lack of harmony or harmony than another?”

Simmias: “No more indeed.”

Socrates: “And in that condition, would one soul share more in vice or virtue than another, if vice is lack of harmony and virtue is harmony?”

Simmias: “No more.”

94a

Socrates: “Rather, Simmias, by a sound argument, no soul will share in vice at all, if it is a harmony. For a harmony, being entirely harmony, could never share in lack of harmony.”

Simmias: “No, indeed.”

Socrates: “Nor, surely, could a soul, being entirely soul, share in vice.”

Simmias: “How could it, given what we said before?”

Socrates: “Then by this argument all the souls of all living things will be equally good, if all souls are naturally souls in the same way.”

Simmias: “That seems so to me, Socrates.”

94b

Socrates: “And does it seem right to you for the argument to say that, and to have these consequences, if the hypothesis that the soul is a harmony were correct?”

Simmias: “Not in the least.”

Socrates: “Now, of all the things in a human being, do you say that anything else rules, except the soul—especially when it is wise?”

Simmias: “I do not.”

Socrates: “Does it yield to the body's affections, or oppose them? I mean, for instance, when heat and thirst are present, it pulls in the opposite direction and does not drink; when hunger is present, it does not eat. We see the soul opposing the body's conditions in countless other ways too, don’t we?”

94c

Simmias: “Very much so.”

Socrates: “And earlier we agreed that a harmony, if it is a harmony, can never sing in opposition to the things that are stretched or slackened or plucked, or that undergo any other condition from which it happens to be composed. It follows those things and never leads them. Is that what we agreed?”

Simmias: “We did. How could we not?”

Socrates: “What, then? Now doesn’t the soul appear to do the complete opposite: to rule all those things from which it is said to be composed, and to oppose them in almost every way throughout life, governing them; sometimes chastising them harshly and with pain, as in exercise and medicine, and sometimes more gently; sometimes threatening, sometimes admonishing, as though it were something different, speaking with desires, anger, and fears? Homer has portrayed something of this in the Odyssey, where he makes Odysseus say—”

94d

Socrates (continuing): “‘He struck his chest and rebuked his heart with these words—’”

94e

Socrates (continuing): “‘Bear up, my heart; you have endured worse than this before.’”

Socrates: “Do you think Homer wrote that with the idea that the soul itself is a harmony, and the sort of thing to be led by the body's affections, rather than something that leads and governs them, and is something far more divine than any harmony?”

Simmias: “By Zeus, Socrates, I think so.”

95a

Socrates: “Then, my good friend, it is in no way right for us to say that the soul is a kind of harmony. For then, it seems, we would agree neither with the divine poet Homer nor with ourselves.”

Simmias: “That is so.”

Socrates: “Well then,” he said, “we have somehow appeased Harmonia of Thebes, it seems, to a reasonable degree. But how shall we appease Cadmus, Cebes, and with what argument?”

95b

Cebes: “I think you will find a way. At any rate, you made the argument against harmony wonderfully, beyond what I expected. When Simmias was speaking about his difficulty, I was amazed whether anyone could make use of his argument. So I found it quite surprising when it did not withstand the first approach of your argument. I would not be surprised if Cadmus’ argument met the same fate.”

Socrates: “My good man, don’t boast, lest some ill will turn the argument we are about to make against us. The god will take care of that. But let us come close, as Homer would have us, and test what you say. The heart of what you seek is this: you demand proof that our soul is indestructible and immortal, if a philosopher who is about to die is to be confident and believe that after death he will fare especially well there, rather than ending his life after living some other kind of life.”

95c

Socrates (continuing): “Showing that the soul is strong, divine-like, and existed earlier, before we were born human beings, does not, you say, prove it immortal. It shows only that the soul has lasted a long time, existed for an immeasurably long time before, and knew and did many things. Yet it was no more immortal for that.”

95d

Socrates (continuing): “Its very coming into a human body was the beginning of its destruction, like a disease; it would live this life in hardship and, when it ended in what is called death, it would perish. You say it makes no difference, as far as each of us is concerned in fearing death, whether the soul enters a body once or many times. Anyone who is not foolish ought to fear, you say, if he has no knowledge or account to give that the soul is immortal.”

95e

Socrates: “I think that is roughly what you are saying, Cebes. I deliberately go over it again and again so that nothing escapes us; add or remove anything you wish.”

Cebes: “For now, I have nothing to remove or add. What you say is what I mean.”

Socrates: “Then, Cebes, you are asking a substantial question. For we must investigate the cause of generation and destruction as a whole.”

96a

Socrates: “So I will go through my own experiences about these things, if you like. Then, if anything I say seems useful to you for reaching conviction about what you are asking, you can make use of it.”

Cebes: “I would like that.”

Socrates: “Then listen, as if I were going to tell a story. When I was young, Cebes, I had an astonishing desire for the wisdom they call the study of nature. I thought it splendid to know the causes of each thing: why each comes to be, why it perishes, and why it exists. I was constantly turning the questions over in my mind, beginning with matters like these: when hot and cold undergo a kind of putrefaction, as some people say, do living creatures then grow together?”

96b

Socrates (continuing): “Is it blood by which we think, or air, or fire? Or is it none of these, but the brain that supplies the senses of hearing, seeing, and smelling, from which memory and opinion arise, and, when memory and opinion have come to rest, knowledge arises from them? I also considered the destructions of these things, and the conditions of the heavens and the earth.”

96c

Socrates (continuing): “In the end I came to think that I was wholly unsuited to this kind of inquiry. Let me give you a clear proof of it: the things I had once known clearly—even things I and others thought I knew well—I became so completely confused about through this inquiry that I unlearned what I had previously thought I knew, including many other things and why a human being grows. I used to think it obvious to everyone that this happens because of eating and drinking.”

96d

Socrates (continuing): “When flesh is added to flesh from food, and bone to bone, and, in the same way, what belongs to each of the other parts is added to them, then the small volume that was there later becomes large, and in this way a small person becomes large. That is what I thought then. Don’t you think so?”

Cebes: “I do.”

Socrates: “Consider this too. I thought I understood well enough that when a large person stood beside a small one, the large person was larger by a head, and likewise one horse was larger than another by a head.”

96e

Socrates (continuing): “And even clearer than these, I thought ten was more than eight because two had been added to it, and that two cubits were greater than one cubit because they exceeded it by half.”

Cebes: “But what do you think about them now?”

97a

Socrates: “By Zeus, I am far from thinking that I know the cause of any of these things. I do not even accept that, when one is added to one, either the one that it was added to has become two, or the added one, or that the one added and the one it was added to became two because one was added to the other. I am amazed: when each was separate from the other, each was one, and they were not two; but when they came near each other, their coming together and being placed close to one another became the cause of their becoming two.”

97b

Socrates (continuing): “Nor can I any longer be persuaded that, when one is divided, division is the cause of two coming to be. This is the opposite cause of two coming to be from the one: then they were brought near and one was added to the other; now one is taken away and separated from the other. Nor can I persuade myself that I know how one comes to be, or in a word how anything comes to be, perishes, or exists, by this method of inquiry. I mix things up at random in some other way, but I have nothing to do with this method.”

Socrates (continuing): “But once I heard someone reading from a book, as he said, by Anaxagoras—”

97c

Socrates (continuing): “—who said that Mind is what orders everything and is the cause of all things. I was pleased with this cause and thought it was somehow right that Mind should be the cause of all things. I thought that if this were so, Mind, in ordering everything, would arrange each thing in the way that was best for it.”

97d

Socrates (continuing): “So if anyone wanted to find the cause of how each thing comes to be, perishes, or exists, he would have to find what is best for that thing: either for it to be or to undergo or do anything else. A human being should consider nothing else, either about himself or anything else, except what is best and what is better. And the same person must know what is worse, since the same knowledge concerns both.”

97e

Socrates (continuing): “Thinking this over, I was delighted, believing I had found in Anaxagoras a teacher about the cause of existing things, one suited to my own mind. I thought he would first tell me whether the earth is flat or round, and then explain the cause and necessity, telling me what was better and why it was better for the earth to be as it is; if he said it was at the center, he would explain why that was better.”

98a

Socrates (continuing): “If he showed me these things, I was prepared to ask for no other kind of cause. I was equally prepared to ask about the sun, the moon, and the other stars: about their relative speed, their turns, and their other motions—how it is best for each to act and be affected as it is.”

98b

Socrates (continuing): “For since he said they were ordered by Mind, I never thought he would introduce another cause for them than that it was best for them to be as they are. I thought that in assigning the cause to each and to all in common, he would explain both what is best for each and the good shared by all. I would not have sold my hopes for a great deal. I took up his books with the greatest eagerness and read them as quickly as I could, so that I might know as quickly as possible what is better and what is worse.”

Socrates (continuing): “But, my friend, I was swept away from that wonderful hope when, as I went forward in my reading, I saw that the man made no use of Mind at all and assigned no causes to the ordering of things, but instead blamed air, aether, water, and many other strange things—”

98c

Socrates (continuing): “—It seemed to me that he had acted much like someone who said that Socrates does everything he does by Mind, and then, when trying to give the causes of each thing I do, said first that I sit here now because my body is composed of bones and sinews: the bones are solid and have joints separating them, while the sinews can tighten and loosen, and, together with the flesh and skin that hold them together, wrap around the bones.”

98d

Socrates (continuing): “So, as the bones hang in their joints, the sinews loosen or tighten and make it possible for me to bend my limbs. That is why I sit here, bent over. And he might give other such causes for my talking with you, blaming voices, air, hearing, and countless other things, while neglecting to state the causes that are truly causes: that, since the Athenians thought it better to condemn me, I too have thought it better and more just to remain here and undergo the penalty they ordered. For, by the Dog, I think these bones and sinews of mine would long ago have been—”

98e

Socrates (continuing): “—in Megara or among the Boeotians, carried off by the opinion of what was best, if I had not thought it more just and more honorable to pay the penalty imposed by the city rather than flee and run away.”

99a

Socrates: “It is very strange to call things of that sort causes. If someone said that without having bones and sinews, and all the other things I have, I would not be able to do what I decided, he would be speaking truly. But to say that I do what I do because of these things, and that I act intelligently without choosing what is best, would be a great and careless misuse of the argument.”

99b

Socrates (continuing): “For it is to fail to distinguish that one thing is the cause in truth, and another the thing without which the cause could never be a cause. Most people, it seems to me, grope around in the dark, giving it an alien name and calling it a cause. That is why one person puts a whirl around the earth and makes the earth stay in place under the sky; another props it up on air as though on a broad kneading-trough.”

99c

Socrates (continuing): “But they do not search for the power by which things are now arranged in the best possible way, nor do they believe it has any divine strength. They think they could someday find an Atlas stronger and more immortal, who holds everything together more completely; but they do not believe that what is truly good and fitting binds and holds things together. I would gladly become anyone’s student in this kind of cause, whatever it may be. But since I was deprived of it, and was unable either to discover it myself or learn it from someone else, would you like me, Cebes, to demonstrate to you the second sailing I made in search of the cause?”

99d

Cebes: “More than anything, I would.”

Socrates: “After this, then, when I had grown tired of examining the things that are, I thought I should be careful not to suffer what happens to people who look at and examine the sun during an eclipse. Some people ruin their eyes unless they look at its image in water or something of that sort.”

99e

Socrates (continuing): “I thought something like that might happen to me, and I feared that my soul would be utterly blinded if I looked at things directly with my eyes and tried to grasp them with each of my senses. So I thought I should take refuge in arguments and examine the truth of existing things in them.”

100a

Socrates (continuing): “Perhaps the comparison I am making is not quite apt. For I do not at all agree that someone who examines what is by means of arguments examines it more in images than someone who examines it in actions. Still, this is the way I set out. In each case I take as a hypothesis the argument I judge strongest. Whatever seems to me to agree with it I set down as true, both about causes and about everything else; whatever does not, I set down as not true. I want to explain more clearly what I mean, because I think you do not understand me now.”

Cebes: “By Zeus, not very well.”

100b

Socrates (continuing): “I mean nothing new, but what I have kept saying all along, both at other times and in the argument just now. I am going to try to show you the kind of cause I have worked out, and I will go back again to those much discussed things. I begin from the assumption that there is something Beautiful itself by itself, and something Good, something Great, and all the rest. If you grant these and agree that they exist, I hope to show you the cause from them, and to find that the soul is immortal.”

100c

Cebes: “You may be sure I grant it. Go on.”

Socrates: “Then examine what follows from them, and see whether you agree with me. It seems to me that if anything else is beautiful besides the Beautiful itself, it is beautiful for no other reason than that it shares in that Beautiful. I say the same of everything. Do you agree to this kind of cause?”

Cebes: “I do.”

Socrates (continuing): “I still do not understand or know how to recognize these clever causes. If someone tells me—”

100d

Socrates (continuing): “—that anything is beautiful because it has a bright color or shape or anything else of that kind, I dismiss all the rest, because all of it confuses me. I simply, artlessly, and perhaps foolishly hold on to this: nothing else makes it beautiful except the presence of, or sharing in, or whatever the relation may be, of that Beautiful. I no longer insist on the precise nature of that relation; I do insist that all beautiful things are beautiful by the Beautiful. This seems to me the safest answer to give myself or anyone else, and holding to this—”

100e

Socrates (continuing): “—I think I will never fall. It is safe for me, and for anyone else, to answer that beautiful things are beautiful by the Beautiful. Don’t you think so?”

Cebes: “Yes.”

Socrates: “Then large things are large by greatness, and larger things are larger by greatness; smaller things are smaller by smallness?”

Cebes: “Yes.”

Socrates: “So you would not accept it if someone said that one person is greater than another by a head, and that the shorter person is shorter by that same—”

101a

Socrates (continuing): “—head. But you would insist that you mean nothing other than that whatever is greater than something else is greater by nothing other than greatness, and is greater because of greatness; while what is smaller is smaller by nothing other than smallness, and is smaller because of smallness.”

Socrates (continuing): “You would fear, I think, that some opposing argument might confront you if you said someone was greater or smaller by a head: first, that the greater is greater and the smaller smaller by the same thing; then that the greater person is greater by a small head—”

101b

Socrates (continuing): “—that would be a marvel: that something great should be great by something small. Wouldn’t you fear that?”

Cebes: “I would,” he said, laughing.

Socrates: “And wouldn’t you fear saying that ten is more than eight by two, and exceeds it for that reason, rather than by number and because of number? Or that a two-cubit length is greater than a one-cubit length by half of it, rather than by greatness? The same fear applies, I suppose.”

Cebes: “Very much so.”

101c

Socrates (continuing): “And if one were added to one, would you be wary of saying that the addition is the cause of two coming to be? Or if one were split, that the splitting is the cause? You would loudly declare that you know no other way for each thing to come to be than by sharing in the proper being of whatever it shares in. In these cases, you have no other cause for two coming to be than sharing in Twoness; whatever is to become two must share in it, and whatever is to be one must share in Oneness. You would leave those splitting and adding tricks to people wiser than yourself to answer. You, afraid—as the saying goes—”

101d

Socrates (continuing): “—of your own shadow and your inexperience, would cling to the safety of your hypothesis and answer in this way. If someone challenged the hypothesis itself, you would let him be and would not answer until you had examined whether what follows from it agrees with one another or conflicts. And when you had to give an account of the hypothesis itself, you would do the same: take in turn another hypothesis, whichever seemed best among those higher up—”

101e

Socrates (continuing): “—until you reached something adequate. At the same time, you would not muddle everything together, as the debaters do, by discussing both the starting point and what follows from it, if you really wanted to discover something that is. They may not care about any argument or thought of this kind. They are clever enough, with their supposed wisdom, to stir everything up and still satisfy themselves. But if you are a philosopher, I think you would do as I say.”

102a

Simmias and Cebes: “You speak most truly.”

Echecrates: “By Zeus, Phaedo, and rightly so. What he said seems wonderfully clear to me, even to someone with only a little understanding.”

Phaedo: “Quite so, Echecrates. All those present thought the same.”

Echecrates: “And we who were absent think so too, now that we hear it. But what was said after that?”

102b

Phaedo: “As I recall, once they had agreed to this, and it was accepted that each of the Forms exists and that other things, by sharing in these Forms, take their names from them, Socrates next asked: ‘If you say this, then when you say that Simmias is greater than Socrates but smaller than Phaedo, don’t you say that both greatness and smallness are present in Simmias?’”

Cebes: “I do.”

102c

Phaedo (continuing): “ ‘But do you agree that Simmias’ being taller than Socrates is not, as the words say, the truth of the matter? Simmias is not naturally taller because he is Simmias, but because of the height he happens to have. Nor does he exceed Socrates because Socrates is Socrates, but because Socrates has smallness in relation to Simmias’ height.’”

Cebes: “That is true.”

Phaedo (continuing): “ ‘Nor is Phaedo taller than Simmias because Phaedo is Phaedo, but because Phaedo has height in relation to Simmias’ smallness. So Simmias is called both small and great, since he stands between the two: he holds smallness toward the one he exceeds in height—’”

102d

Phaedo (continuing): “ ‘—and offers his height to the other, where it exceeds the smallness. Smiling, Socrates said, “I seem to be speaking like a book, but what I say is nonetheless true.” Cebes agreed. “I say this because I want you to agree with me. It seems to me that not only Greatness itself refuses ever to be great and small at the same time, but also the greatness in us never accepts smallness or consents to be exceeded. It has one of two outcomes: either it flees and withdraws when its opposite, smallness, approaches—”’”

102e

Phaedo (continuing): “ ‘—or, when smallness comes, it is destroyed. It will not stay and accept smallness while remaining what it was. Just as I, having accepted and endured smallness, remain the same person I am and am still this same small person, Greatness has not dared to become small. Likewise, the smallness in us never wishes to become or be great. Nor does any other opposite, while remaining what it was, ever become and be its opposite at the same time. It either goes away or is destroyed in this condition.’”

Cebes: “That seems entirely so to me.”

103a

Phaedo (continuing): “One of those present, on hearing this, said—I do not clearly remember who it was—‘By the gods, isn’t the opposite of what we are saying now what we agreed to in our earlier arguments: that the greater comes to be from the smaller, and the smaller from the greater, and that this is simply how opposites come to be, from opposites? But now it seems to me we are saying that this could never happen.’”

103b

Phaedo (continuing): “Socrates tilted his head toward him and listened. ‘Bravely recalled,’ he said, ‘but you have not noticed the difference between what is being said now and what was said then. Then we said that an opposite thing comes to be from an opposite thing; now we say that the opposite itself could never become opposite to itself, neither the opposite in us nor the opposite in nature. Then, my friend, we were speaking of things that have opposites, and naming them by the name of those opposites; now we are speaking of the opposites themselves, whose presence gives the things their names.’”

103c

Socrates (continuing): “ ‘We say that those opposites themselves would never consent to undergo one another’s coming-to-be.’ He looked toward Cebes and asked, ‘Did anything in what our friend said trouble you too, Cebes?’”

Cebes: “No, not in the same way; though I am not saying that many things do not trouble me.”

Socrates: “Then we have agreed without qualification that an opposite will never be its own opposite?”

Cebes: “Entirely.”

103d

Socrates (continuing): “Now consider this too, and see whether you will agree. Do you call anything hot and anything cold?”

Cebes: “I do.”

Socrates: “Are they the same as fire and snow?”

Cebes: “By Zeus, no.”

Socrates: “So heat is one thing and fire another, and cold is one thing and snow another?”

Cebes: “Yes.”

Socrates: “I think you agree that snow, if it is snow, will never remain what it was—snow and hot—when it receives heat, as we said earlier. As heat approaches, it will either withdraw or be destroyed.”

Cebes: “Certainly.”

103e

Socrates (continuing): “And fire too, when cold approaches it, will either withdraw or be destroyed; it will never endure and accept cold while remaining what it was, fire and cold.”

Cebes: “You speak truly.”

Socrates: “In some such cases, then, not only must the Form itself always have its own name, but also something else that is not the Form itself, yet always has its character whenever it exists. Perhaps I can make this clearer with the following example: the odd must, I suppose, always have the name we now give it.”

104a

Cebes: “Certainly.”

Socrates: “I am asking whether this is true only of Forms, or also of something else that is not the Odd itself, yet must always be called odd along with its own name, because its nature is such that it can never be separated from the odd. I mean, for example, the number three, and many others. Consider three: doesn’t it seem to you that it must always be called both by its own name and by the name ‘odd,’ though the Odd is not the same as the triad?”

104b

Socrates (continuing): “ ‘Still, the triad, the pentad, and every one-and-a-half multiple are naturally odd, although each is not the Odd itself. Likewise, two and four and every other even number are always even, although each is not the Even itself. Do you agree?’”

Cebes: “How could I not?”

Socrates: “Then observe what I want to make clear. It seems that not only do opposite Forms refuse one another, but also those things which are not themselves opposites yet always contain opposites refuse to admit the Form opposite to the one they contain. When that Form approaches, either—”

104c

Socrates (continuing): “ ‘—they are destroyed or withdraw. Won’t we say that the triad must be destroyed or suffer anything rather than remain three and become even?’”

Cebes: “Certainly.”

Socrates: “And two is not the opposite of three.”

Cebes: “No.”

Socrates: “So it is not only opposite Forms that cannot endure one another’s approach; some other things too cannot endure the approach of opposites.”

Cebes: “What you say is most true.”

104d

Socrates (continuing): “Would you like us, if we can, to define what sort of things these are?”

Cebes: “Very much.”

Socrates: “Might they be things that, whatever occupies them, compel it to have not only that thing’s Form but also always the Form of some opposite?”

Cebes: “What do you mean?”

Socrates: “Just what we said a moment ago. Whatever the Form of three occupies must be not only three but also odd.”

Cebes: “Certainly.”

Socrates: “The opposite Form to the one that produces this could never come to the thing of that kind, could it?”

Cebes: “No.”

Socrates: “But it was the odd Form that produced it?”

Cebes: “Yes.”

Socrates: “And the Form of the even is opposite to that?”

Cebes: “Yes.”

104e

Socrates (continuing): “Then the Form of the even will never come to three.”

Cebes: “No.”

Socrates: “So three has no share in the even.”

Cebes: “No share.”

Socrates: “Then the triad is not even.”

Cebes: “It is not.”

105a

Socrates (continuing): “Now, what I wanted to define was this: what things, though not opposite to something, still do not admit it—the opposite. For example, the triad, though it is not opposite to the even, admits it no more than the even does, since it always brings along its opposite; and two brings along the odd, and fire brings along the cold, and many other things do so too. Consider whether you would define them this way: not only does an opposite refuse its opposite, but also whatever brings an opposite to whatever it approaches never admits the opposite of what it brings along.”

105b

Socrates (continuing): “ ‘Let me remind you again; it is no harm to hear something often. Five will not admit the even, nor ten, which is twice five, the odd—even though ten itself is opposite to something else. Nor will one-and-a-half, or the half, admit the whole; nor will a third, or anything of that sort, admit the whole. Do you follow and agree?’”

Cebes: “Very much. I agree and follow.”

Socrates: “Then answer me from the beginning. Don’t answer what I ask; answer by imitating me. I see, from what we are saying now, a different safe answer than the one I first gave. If you asked me what must be present in a body for it to be hot, I would not give you—”

105c

Socrates (continuing): “ ‘—that safe but ignorant answer, “heat,” but a more refined answer, based on what we are saying now: “fire.” Nor, if you asked what must be present in a body for it to be ill, would I say “illness,” but “fever.” And if you asked what must be present in a number for it to be odd, I would not say “oddness,” but “one.” The same applies to all the others. See if you now understand well enough what I mean.’”

Cebes: “Quite well.”

Socrates: “Then answer: what must be present in a body for it to be alive?”

Cebes: “Soul.”

105d

Socrates (continuing): “Is that always so?”

Cebes: “How could it not be?”

Socrates: “Then whatever the soul occupies, it always comes to it bringing life?”

Cebes: “It does.”

Socrates: “Is there anything opposite to life, or nothing?”

Cebes: “There is.”

Socrates: “What?”

Cebes: “Death.”

Socrates: “Then, as we agreed from our earlier discussion, the soul will never admit the opposite of what it always brings?”

Cebes: “Very strongly agreed.”

Socrates: “What did we just call what does not admit the Form of the even?”

Cebes: “Uneven.”

105e

Socrates (continuing): “And what does not admit justice, or what does not admit music?”

Cebes: “Unjust, and unmusical.”

Socrates: “Good. And what do we call what does not admit death?”

Cebes: “Deathless.”

Socrates: “Then the soul does not admit death?”

Cebes: “No.”

Socrates: “Then the soul is deathless.”

Cebes: “It is deathless.”

Socrates: “So shall we say that this has been proved, or what do you think?”

Cebes: “Quite sufficiently, Socrates.”

106a

Socrates (continuing): “Then what, Cebes? If the uneven had to be imperishable, would three be anything other than imperishable?”

Cebes: “How could it be?”

Socrates: “And if the non-hot had to be imperishable, then whenever someone brought heat to snow, the snow would withdraw whole and unmelted. It would not be destroyed, nor would it remain and accept the heat.”

Cebes: “You speak truly.”

Socrates: “In the same way, I think, if what is not-cold were imperishable, then whenever something cold approached fire, the fire would not be extinguished or destroyed. It would leave intact and go away.”

Cebes: “Necessarily.”

106b

Socrates (continuing): “So must we speak in the same way about the deathless? If the deathless is also imperishable, then when death approaches the soul, it cannot be destroyed. For from what we said earlier, it will not admit death or be dead, just as three, we said, will not be even; nor will the odd, nor fire be cold, nor the heat in fire.”

Socrates (continuing): “ ‘But,’ someone might say, ‘what prevents the odd from not becoming even when the even approaches, as we agreed, but from being destroyed, and an even thing coming to be in its place?’ We could not argue against someone who said this by claiming that the odd is not destroyed, since the uneven is not imperishable. If we had agreed that it was, we could easily have argued that when the even approaches, the odd and the three go away. We could make the same argument about fire, heat, and the rest. Isn’t that so?”

Cebes: “Certainly.”

106c

Socrates (continuing): “ ‘Now too, if we agree that the deathless is also imperishable, then the soul is not only deathless but imperishable. If we do not, we need another argument.’”

Cebes: “There is no need for that, at least on this point. For hardly anything else would escape destruction, if the deathless itself, which is eternal, is going to admit destruction.”

106d

Socrates (continuing): “I think everyone would agree that a god, the Form of Life itself, and anything else that is deathless, can never be destroyed.”

Cebes: “By Zeus, all human beings would agree, and even more so, I think, the gods.”

Socrates: “Since the deathless is also imperishable, must not the soul, if it happens to be deathless, also be imperishable?”

Cebes: “Necessarily.”

106e

Socrates (continuing): “Then when death comes upon a human being, the mortal part of him, it seems, dies, while the deathless part goes away whole and undestroyed, withdrawing before death.”

Cebes: “It appears so.”

Socrates: “So, Cebes, it is beyond all doubt that the soul is deathless and—”

107a

Socrates (continuing): “—imperishable, and our souls will truly exist in Hades.”

Cebes: “For my part, Socrates, I have nothing further to say against this, nor any way to distrust the arguments. But if Simmias here, or anyone else, has anything to say, it would be good not to keep silent. For I do not know to what other occasion someone could put off speaking or listening about such matters, if he wants to do either.”

Simmias: “I too have no further ground for distrust, given what has been said. Still, because of the greatness of the matters the arguments concern—”

107b

Simmias (continuing): “—and because I hold human weakness in low esteem, I am forced to retain some distrust in myself about what has been said.”

Socrates: “Not only is that well said, Simmias; even our first assumptions, if you trust them, must still be examined more clearly. If you work them through sufficiently, I think you will follow the argument as far as a human being can follow it. And if that itself becomes clear, you will seek nothing further.”

Simmias: “You speak truly.”

107c

Socrates (continuing): “But, gentlemen, we should reflect on this: if the soul is immortal, then it needs care not only for the time we call life, but for all time. The danger now may indeed seem terrible if someone neglects it. If death were release from everything, it would be a godsend for the wicked to die and be rid at once of their body and their wickedness along with their soul. But now, since the soul appears to be immortal, it can have no other—”

107d

Socrates (continuing): “—escape from evils or salvation except becoming as good and wise as possible. For the soul arrives in Hades carrying nothing else but its education and upbringing, things said to be of the greatest help or harm to the dead person right at the start of the journey there. The story goes like this: when each person dies, the guide who had been assigned to him in life tries to lead him to a certain place, where those gathered must undergo judgment and then travel to Hades with that guide who has been appointed to escort them from here to there.”

107e

Socrates (continuing): “Once they have arrived there and received what they must receive, and remained for the appointed time, another guide brings them back here, after long periods of time and many cycles.”

108a

Socrates (as reported by Phaedo): “So the journey is not as Aeschylus’ Telephus describes it. He says that a single road leads to Hades. It seems to me that it is neither simple nor one. Otherwise there would be no need for guides: no one would miss the way if there were only one. As it is, it seems to have many forks and crossroads. I infer this from the sacrifices and rites here. The orderly and wise soul follows its guide and is not ignorant of what lies ahead. But a soul attached to the body by desire, as I said earlier, and long distraught about that and the visible region—”

108b

Socrates (continuing): “—after much resistance and suffering, is led away forcibly and with difficulty by the spirit appointed to guide it. When it arrives where the other souls are, if it is impure and has done something of that sort—if it has been involved in unjust killings or has committed other such deeds, kin to these and the work of souls of the same kind—everyone flees and turns aside from it. No one is willing to travel with it or be its guide.”

108c

Socrates (continuing): “It wanders by itself in complete perplexity until a certain time has passed. When that time comes, it is carried by necessity to the dwelling suited to it. But a soul that has passed its life in purity and moderation finds gods as companions and guides, and each soul settles in the place appropriate to it. There are many wonderful places on the earth itself, and the earth is neither of the kind nor of the size that people accustomed to speak about it suppose, as I have been persuaded by someone.”

108d

Simmias: “How do you mean this, Socrates? I too have heard a great deal about the earth, but not what has persuaded you. I would gladly hear it.”

Socrates: “Simmias, I do not think Glaucus’ skill would be needed to describe what it is actually like. To describe it as true seems harder than Glaucus’ art; and perhaps I could not do it. Even if I knew how, I think my life would not be long enough for so lengthy an account. Still, nothing prevents me from telling you the shape of the earth as I believe it to be—”

108e

Socrates (continuing): “—and its places, as I believe they are.”

Simmias: “But these things too will suffice.”

Socrates: “Well, I am persuaded first that, if the earth is spherical and lies at the center of the heavens, it needs neither air to keep it from falling nor any other such necessity—”

109a

Socrates (continuing): “—the likeness of the heavens to themselves on every side and the earth’s own balance are enough to hold it. For a balanced thing placed at the center of something uniform cannot incline more in one direction than another; remaining evenly poised, it will stay without leaning. This is the first thing I am persuaded of.”

Simmias: “And rightly so.”

Socrates: “Further, I am persuaded that the earth is immensely large, and that we live—”

109b

Socrates (continuing): “—from the Phasis to the Pillars of Heracles occupy only a small part of it, living around the sea as ants or frogs live around a pond. Many other people live elsewhere, in many such regions. For there are hollows of every kind and size all around the earth, into which water, mist, and air have flowed together. The earth itself lies pure in the pure heaven where the stars are, the heaven most people who discuss these things call ‘aether.’”

109c

Socrates (continuing): “These things are its sediment, flowing continually into the hollows of the earth. We live in those hollows without realizing it, and suppose we live on the upper surface of the earth. It is as if someone living at the bottom in the middle of the sea thought he lived on the sea’s surface: seeing the sun and the other stars through the water, he would take the sea for the sky, but—”

109d

Socrates (continuing): “—because of his slowness and weakness he would never have reached the sea’s edge or seen, after coming out of the water and raising his head into this region, how much purer and more beautiful it is than the place where he lives. Nor would he have heard from anyone who had seen it. We have undergone the same thing: living in a hollow of the earth, we suppose we live on its upper surface and call the air ‘the heaven,’ as though the stars moved through it. But the truth is the same—”

109e

Socrates (continuing): “—because of our weakness and slowness, we cannot pass through the air to its outermost limit. For if someone reached its edge, or grew wings and flew up, he would raise his head and see what is there—just as fish here, when they leap up from the sea, see what is here. If his nature could endure the sight, he would recognize that this is the true heaven, the true light—”

110a

Socrates (continuing): “—and the true earth.”

Socrates: “For this earth here, its stones, and the whole region are spoiled and eaten away, as things in the sea are by salt. Nothing worthwhile grows in the sea, and nothing there is, so to speak, fully formed. There are clefts, sand, immeasurable mud, and mire wherever there is land; none of it is in any way worthy to be compared with the beauties among us. But those things there would appear to differ from ours by far more still—”

110b

Socrates (continuing): “—if it is pleasant to tell a story, Simmias, it is worth hearing what things are on the earth beneath the heaven.”

Simmias: “By all means, Socrates. We would gladly hear this story.”

Socrates: “It is said, my friend, that the earth itself looks like a ball made of twelve pieces of leather, variegated and divided into colors. The colors here are samples of those colors, which painters use. There the whole earth is made of such colors—”

110c

Socrates (continuing): “—and of colors far brighter and purer than these. One part is purple, wonderful in beauty; another is golden; another is whiter than gypsum or snow. It is composed in the same way of the other colors, and of colors more numerous and beautiful than any we have seen. Even its hollows, filled with water and air—”

110d

Socrates (continuing): “—have a kind of color of their own, gleaming amid the variety of the other colors, so that the whole presents a single continuous play of color. Since it is like this, whatever grows there grows correspondingly: trees, flowers, and fruits. The mountains too, and the stones, are more beautiful in their smoothness, transparency, and color. The stones we value here—carnelian, jasper, emerald—”

110e

Socrates (continuing): “—and all such things. There, every stone is like these, and even more beautiful. The reason is that those stones are pure, not eaten away or spoiled as ours are by rot and saltwater. The same is true of the precious stones and all the things we prize: they are fragments of the stones there. And the earth itself is adorned with all these things, and also with gold, silver, and—”

111a

Socrates (continuing): “—the other such things. They are visible there, for they are numerous, large, and spread everywhere over the earth, so that it is a sight for blessed spectators. There are many other living creatures on it, and human beings too: some dwell inland, others around the air as we dwell around the sea, and others on islands near the mainland, with air flowing around them. In a word, what water and sea are for our use, that there—”

111b

Socrates (continuing): “—is air for them, and what air is for us, aether is for them. Their seasons are so tempered that they are free from disease and live much longer than people here. Their sight, hearing, thought, and every such capacity surpass ours by the same measure as air surpasses water and aether surpasses air in purity. They have sacred groves and temples in which gods truly dwell; they hear their voices, receive oracles and visions of them, and converse with them face to face—”

111c

Socrates (continuing): “They see the sun, moon, and stars as they truly are, and their blessedness in every other respect follows from this. The earth as a whole is like this, and the regions around it. But many hollows lie all around it. Some are deeper and more open than the region we inhabit; some are deeper but have a smaller chasm than ours—”

111d

Socrates (continuing): “—and some are shallower than our region but wider. All these hollows are pierced in many places beneath the earth and connected with one another; they have narrower and wider passages through which much water flows from one to another, as if into mixing bowls. Beneath the earth are immeasurable sizes of everlasting rivers, hot and cold waters, much fire and great rivers of fire, and many streams of liquid mud, both purer and more muddy—”

111e

Socrates (continuing): “—like the mud rivers in Sicily before a lava flow, and the lava stream itself. Each region is filled by these streams as the current reaches it. All these things are moved up and down by a kind of oscillation within the earth. This oscillation has a natural cause of the following sort.”

112a

Socrates (continuing): “One of the earth’s chasms is exceptionally large and pierced right through the whole earth. This is the chasm Homer describes as ‘far away, where beneath the earth lies the deepest pit.’ He and many other poets have elsewhere called it Tartarus. All the rivers flow together into this chasm and flow out from it again. Each becomes the kind of stream that corresponds to the region through which it flows.”

112b

Socrates (continuing): “The reason all the streams flow out from there and flow back in is that this liquid mass has no bottom or base. It hangs and surges up and down, and the air and wind around it do the same. The wind follows the water whenever it rushes toward either side of the earth. Just as the breath of those breathing is always flowing out and in, so there the wind, rising and falling with the water, produces terrible and immense gusts as it flows in and out—”

112c

Socrates (continuing): “—When the water withdraws into the place called the lower region, it flows through the earth’s passages into the streams in that region and fills them, as people pump water into reservoirs. When it leaves that region and rushes this way, it fills the streams here in turn. Once these are full, they flow through channels and through the earth, each arriving at the places to which its route leads, making seas, lakes, rivers, and springs. From there they sink again beneath the earth—”

112d

Socrates (continuing): “—some traveling around longer and more numerous regions, others fewer and shorter ones, and they flow back into Tartarus, some much lower than the place from which they were pumped, others only a little. All flow back beneath the point of outflow; some opposite the place from which they emerged, others on the same side. Some make a complete circuit, winding around the earth once or many times, like snakes, and sink as far down as they can before flowing back in.”

112e

Socrates (continuing): “They can descend on either side as far as the middle, but no farther. The region on either side slopes upward for both streams. Among the other rivers are many great and varied currents. Among all these there happen to be four particular streams. The largest and outermost, flowing in a circle, is called Oceanus. Opposite it and flowing in the contrary direction is Acheron, which runs through deserted places—”

113a

Socrates (continuing): “—and, running beneath the earth, reaches the Acherusian Lake. The souls of most of the dead arrive there. After remaining for their appointed periods, some longer and some shorter, they are sent back again into the births of living things. A third river flows out between these, and near its outlet it falls into a great place burning with much fire, making a lake larger than our sea, boiling with water and mud. From there—”

113b

Socrates (continuing): “—it flows in a circle, turbid and muddy, winds around the earth, and reaches other places, including the outer edge of the Acherusian Lake. It does not mix with the lake’s water. After winding around many times beneath the earth, it empties into the lower part of Tartarus. This is the river they call Pyriphlegethon; its branches throw up fragments of lava wherever they reach the earth. Opposite it, a fourth river first falls into a fearful and savage place, as the story goes, all dark blue in color—”

113c

Socrates (continuing): “—which they call the Stygian region, and the lake formed by the river’s flow is called Styx. After falling into it and taking on terrible powers in the water, the river sinks beneath the earth, circles around, and flows in the opposite direction to Pyriphlegethon. It meets that river from the opposite side at the Acherusian Lake. Its water too mixes with no other; after circling around, it empties into Tartarus opposite Pyriphlegethon. The poets call it Cocytus.”

113d

Socrates (continuing): “When the dead arrive at the place to which each is carried by his guide, they are first judged: those who have lived well and reverently, and those who have not. Those judged to have lived an ordinary life travel to Acheron. They board the vessels provided for them and arrive at the lake. There they dwell and are purified; they pay the penalty for any wrong they have done and are released, while for their good deeds each receives honors according to their deserts—”

113e

Socrates (continuing): “—but those judged incurable because of the magnitude of their offenses—those who committed many great acts of sacrilege, unjust and lawless murders, or other such crimes—are thrown by their proper fate into Tartarus, from which they never emerge.”

114a

Socrates (continuing): “Those who committed great but curable offenses—for example, doing violence to a father or mother in anger and then living the rest of their lives in remorse, or becoming killers in some other such way—must also fall into Tartarus. But after they have fallen there and spent a year, the wave casts them out: the murderers along Cocytus, those who attacked their parents along Pyriphlegethon. When they are carried to the Acherusian Lake, they cry out and call upon the people they killed or wronged, begging and pleading—”

114b

Socrates (continuing): “—with those people to let them step out into the lake and receive them. If they persuade them, they come out and their sufferings end. If not, they are carried back again to Tartarus, and from there into the rivers; they suffer this until they persuade those they wronged. The judges imposed this penalty on them.”

114c

Socrates (continuing): “Those judged to have excelled in reverent living are released and freed from these regions within the earth, as from prisons. They arrive above in the pure dwelling and settle on the earth. Among these, those who have purified themselves sufficiently through philosophy live entirely without bodies for all the time to come, and arrive at dwellings even more beautiful than these. It is not easy to describe them, and the time we have now is not enough.”

“For all these reasons, Simmias, we must do everything we can during life to share in virtue and wisdom. The prize is beautiful, and the hope is great.”

114d

“A sensible man should not insist that these things are exactly as I have described them. But that this, or something of the sort, is true of our souls and their dwellings—since the soul appears to be immortal—seems to me fitting and worth the risk for someone who believes it to be so. For the risk is beautiful. One should repeat such things to oneself as a spell. That is why I have drawn out this story for so long. For these reasons one should be confident about his soul—”

114e

Socrates (continuing): “—the man who, during life, has let the other pleasures of the body and its adornments go, as things alien to him, and has judged them more likely to do harm than good; while he has devoted himself to the pleasures of learning, and adorned his soul not with an alien ornament but with its own: self-control, justice, courage, freedom, and truth. Such a man waits for his journey to Hades.”

115a

Socrates (continuing): “You, then, Simmias and Cebes and all the rest, will each make your journey later, at some other time. But my own fate, as a tragic poet might say, is calling me now, and it is almost time for me to turn to the bath. I think it is better to drink the drug after bathing, and not to trouble the women with washing my body when I am dead.”

115b

Crito: “All right, Socrates. What instructions do you have for me or the others about your children or anything else? What could we do to please you most?”

Socrates: “Just what I always say, Crito; nothing new: if you take care of yourselves, whatever you do will be welcome to me, to my family, and to yourselves, even if you do not promise it now. But if you neglect yourselves and are unwilling to live, following the tracks of what we have just said and what we said earlier—”

115c

Socrates (continuing): “—then even if you make many solemn promises now, you will accomplish nothing more.”

Crito: “We will try to do this. But how should we bury you?”

Socrates: “However you wish, if you can catch me and I do not escape you.”

He laughed quietly, looked toward us, and said: “Men, I cannot persuade Crito that I am this Socrates here, the one now conversing with you and arranging each part of the discussion—”

115d

Socrates (continuing): “—but he thinks I am the one he will see shortly as a corpse, and he asks how he should bury me. I have spent a long time explaining that, after I drink the drug, I will no longer remain with you, but go away to some blessed state of the fortunate. It seems to me he thinks I say all that for nothing, to comfort both you and myself. So give Crito a pledge on my behalf, the opposite pledge to the one he gave the judges. He pledged that I would remain; you pledge that I will not remain after I die, but will go away—”

115e

Socrates (continuing): “—so that Crito will bear it more easily, and when he sees my body being burned or buried he will not be angry on my behalf, as though I were suffering terrible things. Nor should he say at my funeral that he is laying out Socrates, carrying him out, or burying him. Know this well, my dear Crito: speaking badly is not only a fault in itself; it also does harm to souls.”

Socrates (continuing): “But you should be confident and say that you are burying my body, and bury it—”

116a

Socrates (continuing, as reported by Phaedo): “—in whatever way you like and think most customary.”

Phaedo (continuing): After saying this, he got up and went into a room to bathe, and Crito followed him. He told us to wait. So we stayed, talking among ourselves about what had been said and reviewing it; then again we spoke about the calamity that had come upon us, feeling exactly as though we had been deprived of a father and would spend the rest of our lives as orphans.

116b

Phaedo (continuing): When he had bathed and his children were brought to him—for he had two little sons and one grown son—and the women of his household had arrived, he spoke with them in Crito’s presence and gave them whatever instructions he wished. Then he told the women and children to go away, and came to us. It was already close to sunset, for he had spent a long time inside. He came in, sat down after bathing, and spoke with us for a little while. Then the attendant of the Eleven came and stood beside him.

116c

Attendant: “Socrates, I will not blame you for what I blame other men: they get angry with me and curse me when I tell them, under orders from the officials, to drink the poison. But I have come to know you in this time as the finest, gentlest, and best man of all who have ever come here. And now I know very well that you are not angry with me, since you know who is responsible, but with them. So now, farewell, and try to bear what must be as easily as you can.”

As he said this, he burst into tears, turned away, and left.

116d

Phaedo (continuing): Socrates looked up at him and said, “And you, too, farewell; we will do this.” Then, turning to us, he said, “What a fine fellow! All this time he has come to visit me and sometimes talked with me; he was the best of men. And now, how nobly he weeps for me! Come, Crito, let us obey him. Let someone bring the poison if it is already ground; if not, let the man grind it.”

116e

Crito: “But I think, Socrates, the sun is still on the hills and has not yet set. And I know that others drink very late after the order is given: they dine and drink well, and some even spend time with whomever they happen to desire. Do not hurry; there is still time.”

117a

Socrates: “It is reasonable, Crito, that those people you mention do this, since they think they will gain something by it. And it is reasonable that I will not do so: I think I would gain nothing by drinking a little later, except to make myself ridiculous in my own eyes for clinging to life and sparing something when nothing is left. Come, obey, and do not act otherwise.”

Phaedo (continuing): Crito nodded to the boy standing nearby. The boy went out, and after spending quite some time away, returned with the man who was to give the poison, carrying it ground in a cup.

Phaedo (continuing): When Socrates saw the man, he said, “Well, my good man, you know about these things. What should one do?”

117b

Attendant: “Nothing but drink it, then walk around until your legs feel heavy; after that lie down. That is how it will work.”

Phaedo (continuing): As he spoke, the attendant offered the cup to Socrates. He took it, Echecrates, quite cheerfully, without trembling or changing color or expression. Looking up at the man from beneath his brows, as was his way, he asked, “What do you say about this drink? Is it permitted to pour a little out for someone, or not?”

Attendant: “Socrates, we grind only as much as we think is the proper amount to drink.”

117c

Socrates: “I understand. But surely one may and should pray to the gods that the passage from here to there turn out fortunate. This is what I pray, and may it be so.”

Phaedo (continuing): With these words, he held the cup to his lips and drank it down, very readily and easily. Most of us had been able to keep from weeping reasonably well until then; but when we saw him drinking, and after he had drunk, we could do so no longer. My own tears came pouring out despite myself, so I covered my face and wept for myself—for it was not him I was weeping for, but my own fortune, now deprived of such a companion.

117d

Phaedo (continuing): Crito had risen even before I did, unable to hold back his tears. Apollodorus had been weeping the whole time before this too; then he burst out into a loud wail, crying and grieving, and made everyone there weep except Socrates himself.

Socrates: “What are you doing, you extraordinary people? I sent the women away especially for this reason: that they not make such a scene. For I have heard one should meet death with words of good omen. Be quiet and stand firm.”

117e

Phaedo (continuing): When we heard him, we were ashamed and stopped crying. He walked around; then, when he said his legs were growing heavy, he lay down on his back, as the man had instructed. The man who had given him the poison examined his feet and legs, waiting a while. Then he pressed his foot hard and asked whether he could feel it. Socrates said he could not.

118a

Phaedo (continuing): Then the man examined his shins in turn, and, moving upward in this way, showed us that his body was growing cold and stiff. Socrates himself touched it and said that when the cold reached his heart, he would be gone.

Phaedo (continuing): The region around his belly was now almost entirely cold. He uncovered himself—for he had been covered—and said, as these were his last words: “Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius. Pay the debt, and do not neglect it.”

“It shall be done,” said Crito. “See if you have anything else to say.”

Phaedo (continuing): When Crito asked this, Socrates made no reply. After a short while he moved; the man uncovered him, and his eyes were fixed. Seeing this, Crito closed his mouth and eyes.

Phaedo (continuing): That, Echecrates, was the end of our companion: a man who, as we would say, was the best of all those we had tested at that time, and in every other way the wisest and most just.
