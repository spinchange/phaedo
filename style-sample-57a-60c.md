# Style Sample: *Phaedo* 57a–60c

### 57a

**Echecrates:** Phaedo, were you there yourself with Socrates on the day he drank the drug in prison, or did you hear it from someone else?

**Phaedo:** I was there myself, Echecrates.

**Echecrates:** Then what did the man say before his death, and how did he meet it? I would gladly hear. These days hardly any of the people of Phlius go to Athens, and no visitor has come from there for a long time who could give us a clear account of these events. We have heard only that he died after drinking poison; nothing else could anyone tell us.

### 57b

### 58a

**Phaedo:** So you did not even learn how the trial went?

**Echecrates:** Yes, someone told us that. We were surprised that he seems to have died so long after the trial, though the trial had taken place quite some time earlier. What was the reason, Phaedo?

**Phaedo:** A chance circumstance, Echecrates. It happened that on the day before the trial the stern of the ship the Athenians send to Delos had been garlanded.

**Echecrates:** What does that mean?

**Phaedo:** This is the ship, as the Athenians tell it, in which Theseus once sailed to Crete, taking those famous seven pairs, and saved them—and was saved himself. They say that the Athenians then vowed to Apollo that, if they were saved, they would send a sacred delegation to Delos every year. From that time to this, they send it to the god each year, as they still do. Once the delegation’s voyage begins, they have a law that the city must remain pure throughout that period, and no one may be put to death by public authority until the ship has gone to Delos and returned here. Sometimes this takes a long time, whenever winds happen to hold them back.

### 58b

**Phaedo (continuing):** The voyage begins when Apollo’s priest garlands the ship’s stern. That, as I say, happened by chance on the day before the trial. This is why a long time passed for Socrates in prison between the trial and his death.

### 58c

**Echecrates:** And what about the death itself, Phaedo? What was said and done? Which of the man’s friends were there? Or did the officials not allow them to be present, and did he die without friends around him?

### 58d

**Phaedo:** Not at all. Some were there—in fact, many.

**Echecrates:** Then please do your best to tell us everything as clearly as you can, unless something is keeping you busy.

**Phaedo:** I am free, and I’ll try to tell you. Remembering Socrates—whether speaking myself or listening to someone else—is always the greatest pleasure for me.

**Echecrates:** And you have listeners like that, Phaedo. So try to go through everything as carefully as you can.

### 58e

**Phaedo:** I had a remarkable experience while I was there. I felt no pity, as one might expect to feel when present at the death of a close friend. The man seemed happy to me, Echecrates, in his manner and in his words, so fearlessly and nobly did he meet his end. I felt that even on his way to Hades he was not going without divine favor, and that once there he would fare well, as surely as anyone ever has.

### 59a

**Phaedo (continuing):** So I felt no great sorrow, as one might reasonably expect at a time of mourning; nor, on the other hand, did I feel the pleasure we usually feel when we are engaged in philosophy—as our conversation was, in fact, philosophical. Instead, I was gripped by a very strange feeling, an unusual mixture of pleasure and pain together, as I thought that he would soon die. Much the same was true of all of us there: at one moment we laughed, at another we wept. One of us in particular, Apollodorus—you know the man and his ways—

### 59b

**Echecrates:** Of course.

**Phaedo:** He was completely overcome like that; and I myself was shaken, as were the rest.

**Echecrates:** Who happened to be there, Phaedo?

**Phaedo:** Apollodorus was there, along with the local men Critobulus and his father, Hermogenes, Epigenes, Aeschines, and Antisthenes. Ctesippus of Paeania was there too, and Menexenus, and some others from around here. Plato, I think, was ill.

**Echecrates:** Were any visitors there?

### 59c

**Phaedo:** Yes: Simmias from Thebes, Cebes, Phaedondes, and the two men from Megara, Euclides and Terpsion.

**Echecrates:** What about Aristippus and Cleombrotus? Were they there?

**Phaedo:** No. They were said to be in Aegina.

**Echecrates:** Was anyone else there?

**Phaedo:** I think that was just about everyone.

**Echecrates:** So then, what do you say the conversation was?

**Phaedo:** I’ll try to tell you the whole story from the beginning. In the days before, it had been our custom for me and the others to visit Socrates, meeting early at the courthouse where the trial had taken place, since it was close to the prison. Each time we waited until the prison opened, passing the time together; it did not open early. When it did open, we went in to Socrates and spent most of the day with him. That morning, too, we had gathered earlier than usual.

### 59d

**Phaedo (continuing):** For the evening before, when we left the prison, we learned that the ship had come back from Delos. So we told one another to come to our usual place as early as possible. We arrived, and the doorkeeper—the same man who usually answered the door—came out and told us to wait and not go in until he gave the word. “The Eleven are freeing Socrates,” he said, “and telling him that he is to die today.”

### 59e

### 60a

**Phaedo (continuing):** He did not keep us waiting long. He came back and told us to go in. When we entered, we found Socrates just released from his chains. Xanthippe—you know her—was sitting beside him with their child in her arms. When she saw us, she cried out and said the sort of thing women commonly say: “Socrates, this is the last time your friends will speak with you, and you with them.” Socrates looked at Crito and said, “Crito, have someone take her home.” Some of Crito’s people led her away, crying out and beating her breast.

### 60b

**Phaedo (continuing):** Socrates sat up on the bed, bent his leg, and rubbed it with his hand. As he rubbed it, he said, “How strange this thing is, men, that people call pleasure! How wonderfully it is related to what seems to be its opposite, pain: neither wants to come to a person at the same time as the other, but if someone pursues and catches one, he is almost always forced to catch the other as well—as if the two were joined at a single head, though they are two.”

### 60c

**Phaedo (continuing):** “I think that if Aesop had noticed this, he would have made up a fable: the god wanted to reconcile them while they were fighting, but since he could not, he joined their heads together. That is why, whenever one comes to someone, the other follows afterward. It seems the same thing has happened to me: the pain in my leg, brought on by the fetter, is now followed, it seems, by pleasure.”

Cebes broke in: “By Zeus, Socrates, you did well to remind me. There is something I wanted to ask you about…”

## Terminology and style note

The voices are conversational and distinct: Echecrates asks direct questions, while Phaedo gives a measured eyewitness account. Sentences are kept relatively short where the Greek permits, with longer sentences retained when they carry a sequence of reasoning or narrative detail. Dialogue uses speaker labels and quotation marks; a repeated speaker is marked “continuing” where a Stephanus boundary falls within the same turn.

Recurring terms are rendered by context rather than forced into one English equivalent: **logos** here is “conversation” or “conversation was philosophical,” not mechanically “argument”; **hēdy** is “pleasure,” **lypēron** “pain,” and **philosophia** “philosophy.” “The Eleven” preserves the name of the Athenian officials, and “sacred delegation” conveys *theōria* in its Delian ritual setting. “Poison” renders *pharmakon* in Echecrates’ report; the passage itself later refers to the drug in context.
