### 100a

**Socrates (continuing):** “Perhaps the comparison I am making is not quite apt. For I do not at all agree that someone who examines what is by means of arguments examines it more in images than someone who examines it in actions. Still, this is the way I set out. In each case I take as a hypothesis the argument I judge strongest. Whatever seems to me to agree with it I set down as true, both about causes and about everything else; whatever does not, I set down as not true. I want to explain more clearly what I mean, because I think you do not understand me now.”

**Cebes:** “By Zeus, not very well.”

### 100b

**Socrates (continuing):** “I mean nothing new, but what I have kept saying all along, both at other times and in the argument just now. I am going to try to show you the kind of cause I have worked out, and I will go back again to those much discussed things. I begin from the assumption that there is something Beautiful itself by itself, and something Good, something Great, and all the rest. If you grant these and agree that they exist, I hope to show you the cause from them, and to find that the soul is immortal.”

### 100c

**Cebes:** “You may be sure I grant it. Go on.”

**Socrates:** “Then examine what follows from them, and see whether you agree with me. It seems to me that if anything else is beautiful besides the Beautiful itself, it is beautiful for no other reason than that it shares in that Beautiful. I say the same of everything. Do you agree to this kind of cause?”

**Cebes:** “I do.”

**Socrates (continuing):** “I still do not understand or know how to recognize these clever causes. If someone tells me—”

### 100d

**Socrates (continuing):** “—that anything is beautiful because it has a bright color or shape or anything else of that kind, I dismiss all the rest, because all of it confuses me. I simply, artlessly, and perhaps foolishly hold on to this: nothing else makes it beautiful except the presence of, or sharing in, or whatever the relation may be, of that Beautiful. I no longer insist on the precise nature of that relation; I do insist that all beautiful things are beautiful by the Beautiful. This seems to me the safest answer to give myself or anyone else, and holding to this—”

### 100e

**Socrates (continuing):** “—I think I will never fall. It is safe for me, and for anyone else, to answer that beautiful things are beautiful by the Beautiful. Don’t you think so?”

**Cebes:** “Yes.”

**Socrates:** “Then large things are large by greatness, and larger things are larger by greatness; smaller things are smaller by smallness?”

**Cebes:** “Yes.”

**Socrates:** “So you would not accept it if someone said that one person is greater than another by a head, and that the shorter person is shorter by that same—”

### 101a

**Socrates (continuing):** “—head. But you would insist that you mean nothing other than that whatever is greater than something else is greater by nothing other than greatness, and is greater because of greatness; while what is smaller is smaller by nothing other than smallness, and is smaller because of smallness.”

**Socrates (continuing):** “You would fear, I think, that some opposing argument might confront you if you said someone was greater or smaller by a head: first, that the greater is greater and the smaller smaller by the same thing; then that the greater person is greater by a small head—”

### 101b

**Socrates (continuing):** “—that would be a marvel: that something great should be great by something small. Wouldn’t you fear that?”

**Cebes:** “I would,” he said, laughing.

**Socrates:** “And wouldn’t you fear saying that ten is more than eight by two, and exceeds it for that reason, rather than by number and because of number? Or that a two-cubit length is greater than a one-cubit length by half of it, rather than by greatness? The same fear applies, I suppose.”

**Cebes:** “Very much so.”

### 101c

**Socrates (continuing):** “And if one were added to one, would you be wary of saying that the addition is the cause of two coming to be? Or if one were split, that the splitting is the cause? You would loudly declare that you know no other way for each thing to come to be than by sharing in the proper being of whatever it shares in. In these cases, you have no other cause for two coming to be than sharing in Twoness; whatever is to become two must share in it, and whatever is to be one must share in Oneness. You would leave those splitting and adding tricks to people wiser than yourself to answer. You, afraid—as the saying goes—”

### 101d

**Socrates (continuing):** “—of your own shadow and your inexperience, would cling to the safety of your hypothesis and answer in this way. If someone challenged the hypothesis itself, you would let him be and would not answer until you had examined whether what follows from it agrees with one another or conflicts. And when you had to give an account of the hypothesis itself, you would do the same: take in turn another hypothesis, whichever seemed best among those higher up—”

### 101e

**Socrates (continuing):** “—until you reached something adequate. At the same time, you would not muddle everything together, as the debaters do, by discussing both the starting point and what follows from it, if you really wanted to discover something that is. They may not care about any argument or thought of this kind. They are clever enough, with their supposed wisdom, to stir everything up and still satisfy themselves. But if you are a philosopher, I think you would do as I say.”

### 102a

**Simmias and Cebes:** “You speak most truly.”

**Echecrates:** “By Zeus, Phaedo, and rightly so. What he said seems wonderfully clear to me, even to someone with only a little understanding.”

**Phaedo:** “Quite so, Echecrates. All those present thought the same.”

**Echecrates:** “And we who were absent think so too, now that we hear it. But what was said after that?”

### 102b

**Phaedo:** “As I recall, once they had agreed to this, and it was accepted that each of the Forms exists and that other things, by sharing in these Forms, take their names from them, Socrates next asked: ‘If you say this, then when you say that Simmias is greater than Socrates but smaller than Phaedo, don’t you say that both greatness and smallness are present in Simmias?’”

**Cebes:** “I do.”

### 102c

**Phaedo (continuing):** “ ‘But do you agree that Simmias’ being taller than Socrates is not, as the words say, the truth of the matter? Simmias is not naturally taller because he is Simmias, but because of the height he happens to have. Nor does he exceed Socrates because Socrates is Socrates, but because Socrates has smallness in relation to Simmias’ height.’”

**Cebes:** “That is true.”

**Phaedo (continuing):** “ ‘Nor is Phaedo taller than Simmias because Phaedo is Phaedo, but because Phaedo has height in relation to Simmias’ smallness. So Simmias is called both small and great, since he stands between the two: he holds smallness toward the one he exceeds in height—’”

### 102d

**Phaedo (continuing):** “ ‘—and offers his height to the other, where it exceeds the smallness. Smiling, Socrates said, “I seem to be speaking like a book, but what I say is nonetheless true.” Cebes agreed. “I say this because I want you to agree with me. It seems to me that not only Greatness itself refuses ever to be great and small at the same time, but also the greatness in us never accepts smallness or consents to be exceeded. It has one of two outcomes: either it flees and withdraws when its opposite, smallness, approaches—”’”

### 102e

**Phaedo (continuing):** “ ‘—or, when smallness comes, it is destroyed. It will not stay and accept smallness while remaining what it was. Just as I, having accepted and endured smallness, remain the same person I am and am still this same small person, Greatness has not dared to become small. Likewise, the smallness in us never wishes to become or be great. Nor does any other opposite, while remaining what it was, ever become and be its opposite at the same time. It either goes away or is destroyed in this condition.’”

**Cebes:** “That seems entirely so to me.”

### 103a

**Phaedo (continuing):** “One of those present, on hearing this, said—I do not clearly remember who it was—‘By the gods, isn’t the opposite of what we are saying now what we agreed to in our earlier arguments: that the greater comes to be from the smaller, and the smaller from the greater, and that this is simply how opposites come to be, from opposites? But now it seems to me we are saying that this could never happen.’”

### 103b

**Phaedo (continuing):** “Socrates tilted his head toward him and listened. ‘Bravely recalled,’ he said, ‘but you have not noticed the difference between what is being said now and what was said then. Then we said that an opposite thing comes to be from an opposite thing; now we say that the opposite itself could never become opposite to itself, neither the opposite in us nor the opposite in nature. Then, my friend, we were speaking of things that have opposites, and naming them by the name of those opposites; now we are speaking of the opposites themselves, whose presence gives the things their names.’”

### 103c

**Socrates (continuing):** “ ‘We say that those opposites themselves would never consent to undergo one another’s coming-to-be.’ He looked toward Cebes and asked, ‘Did anything in what our friend said trouble you too, Cebes?’”

**Cebes:** “No, not in the same way; though I am not saying that many things do not trouble me.”

**Socrates:** “Then we have agreed without qualification that an opposite will never be its own opposite?”

**Cebes:** “Entirely.”

### 103d

**Socrates (continuing):** “Now consider this too, and see whether you will agree. Do you call anything hot and anything cold?”

**Cebes:** “I do.”

**Socrates:** “Are they the same as fire and snow?”

**Cebes:** “By Zeus, no.”

**Socrates:** “So heat is one thing and fire another, and cold is one thing and snow another?”

**Cebes:** “Yes.”

**Socrates:** “I think you agree that snow, if it is snow, will never remain what it was—snow and hot—when it receives heat, as we said earlier. As heat approaches, it will either withdraw or be destroyed.”

**Cebes:** “Certainly.”

### 103e

**Socrates (continuing):** “And fire too, when cold approaches it, will either withdraw or be destroyed; it will never endure and accept cold while remaining what it was, fire and cold.”

**Cebes:** “You speak truly.”

**Socrates:** “In some such cases, then, not only must the Form itself always have its own name, but also something else that is not the Form itself, yet always has its character whenever it exists. Perhaps I can make this clearer with the following example: the odd must, I suppose, always have the name we now give it.”

### 104a

**Cebes:** “Certainly.”

**Socrates:** “I am asking whether this is true only of Forms, or also of something else that is not the Odd itself, yet must always be called odd along with its own name, because its nature is such that it can never be separated from the odd. I mean, for example, the number three, and many others. Consider three: doesn’t it seem to you that it must always be called both by its own name and by the name ‘odd,’ though the Odd is not the same as the triad?”

### 104b

**Socrates (continuing):** “ ‘Still, the triad, the pentad, and every one-and-a-half multiple are naturally odd, although each is not the Odd itself. Likewise, two and four and every other even number are always even, although each is not the Even itself. Do you agree?’”

**Cebes:** “How could I not?”

**Socrates:** “Then observe what I want to make clear. It seems that not only do opposite Forms refuse one another, but also those things which are not themselves opposites yet always contain opposites refuse to admit the Form opposite to the one they contain. When that Form approaches, either—”

### 104c

**Socrates (continuing):** “ ‘—they are destroyed or withdraw. Won’t we say that the triad must be destroyed or suffer anything rather than remain three and become even?’”

**Cebes:** “Certainly.”

**Socrates:** “And two is not the opposite of three.”

**Cebes:** “No.”

**Socrates:** “So it is not only opposite Forms that cannot endure one another’s approach; some other things too cannot endure the approach of opposites.”

**Cebes:** “What you say is most true.”

### 104d

**Socrates (continuing):** “Would you like us, if we can, to define what sort of things these are?”

**Cebes:** “Very much.”

**Socrates:** “Might they be things that, whatever occupies them, compel it to have not only that thing’s Form but also always the Form of some opposite?”

**Cebes:** “What do you mean?”

**Socrates:** “Just what we said a moment ago. Whatever the Form of three occupies must be not only three but also odd.”

**Cebes:** “Certainly.”

**Socrates:** “The opposite Form to the one that produces this could never come to the thing of that kind, could it?”

**Cebes:** “No.”

**Socrates:** “But it was the odd Form that produced it?”

**Cebes:** “Yes.”

**Socrates:** “And the Form of the even is opposite to that?”

**Cebes:** “Yes.”

### 104e

**Socrates (continuing):** “Then the Form of the even will never come to three.”

**Cebes:** “No.”

**Socrates:** “So three has no share in the even.”

**Cebes:** “No share.”

**Socrates:** “Then the triad is not even.”

**Cebes:** “It is not.”

### 105a

**Socrates (continuing):** “Now, what I wanted to define was this: what things, though not opposite to something, still do not admit it—the opposite. For example, the triad, though it is not opposite to the even, admits it no more than the even does, since it always brings along its opposite; and two brings along the odd, and fire brings along the cold, and many other things do so too. Consider whether you would define them this way: not only does an opposite refuse its opposite, but also whatever brings an opposite to whatever it approaches never admits the opposite of what it brings along.”

### 105b

**Socrates (continuing):** “ ‘Let me remind you again; it is no harm to hear something often. Five will not admit the even, nor ten, which is twice five, the odd—even though ten itself is opposite to something else. Nor will one-and-a-half, or the half, admit the whole; nor will a third, or anything of that sort, admit the whole. Do you follow and agree?’”

**Cebes:** “Very much. I agree and follow.”

**Socrates:** “Then answer me from the beginning. Don’t answer what I ask; answer by imitating me. I see, from what we are saying now, a different safe answer than the one I first gave. If you asked me what must be present in a body for it to be hot, I would not give you—”

### 105c

**Socrates (continuing):** “ ‘—that safe but ignorant answer, “heat,” but a more refined answer, based on what we are saying now: “fire.” Nor, if you asked what must be present in a body for it to be ill, would I say “illness,” but “fever.” And if you asked what must be present in a number for it to be odd, I would not say “oddness,” but “one.” The same applies to all the others. See if you now understand well enough what I mean.’”

**Cebes:** “Quite well.”

**Socrates:** “Then answer: what must be present in a body for it to be alive?”

**Cebes:** “Soul.”

### 105d

**Socrates (continuing):** “Is that always so?”

**Cebes:** “How could it not be?”

**Socrates:** “Then whatever the soul occupies, it always comes to it bringing life?”

**Cebes:** “It does.”

**Socrates:** “Is there anything opposite to life, or nothing?”

**Cebes:** “There is.”

**Socrates:** “What?”

**Cebes:** “Death.”

**Socrates:** “Then, as we agreed from our earlier discussion, the soul will never admit the opposite of what it always brings?”

**Cebes:** “Very strongly agreed.”

**Socrates:** “What did we just call what does not admit the Form of the even?”

**Cebes:** “Uneven.”

### 105e

**Socrates (continuing):** “And what does not admit justice, or what does not admit music?”

**Cebes:** “Unjust, and unmusical.”

**Socrates:** “Good. And what do we call what does not admit death?”

**Cebes:** “Deathless.”

**Socrates:** “Then the soul does not admit death?”

**Cebes:** “No.”

**Socrates:** “Then the soul is deathless.”

**Cebes:** “It is deathless.”

**Socrates:** “So shall we say that this has been proved, or what do you think?”

**Cebes:** “Quite sufficiently, Socrates.”

### 106a

**Socrates (continuing):** “Then what, Cebes? If the uneven had to be imperishable, would three be anything other than imperishable?”

**Cebes:** “How could it be?”

**Socrates:** “And if the non-hot had to be imperishable, then whenever someone brought heat to snow, the snow would withdraw whole and unmelted. It would not be destroyed, nor would it remain and accept the heat.”

**Cebes:** “You speak truly.”

**Socrates:** “In the same way, I think, if what is not-cold were imperishable, then whenever something cold approached fire, the fire would not be extinguished or destroyed. It would leave intact and go away.”

**Cebes:** “Necessarily.”

### 106b

**Socrates (continuing):** “So must we speak in the same way about the deathless? If the deathless is also imperishable, then when death approaches the soul, it cannot be destroyed. For from what we said earlier, it will not admit death or be dead, just as three, we said, will not be even; nor will the odd, nor fire be cold, nor the heat in fire.”

**Socrates (continuing):** “ ‘But,’ someone might say, ‘what prevents the odd from not becoming even when the even approaches, as we agreed, but from being destroyed, and an even thing coming to be in its place?’ We could not argue against someone who said this by claiming that the odd is not destroyed, since the uneven is not imperishable. If we had agreed that it was, we could easily have argued that when the even approaches, the odd and the three go away. We could make the same argument about fire, heat, and the rest. Isn’t that so?”

**Cebes:** “Certainly.”

### 106c

**Socrates (continuing):** “ ‘Now too, if we agree that the deathless is also imperishable, then the soul is not only deathless but imperishable. If we do not, we need another argument.’”

**Cebes:** “There is no need for that, at least on this point. For hardly anything else would escape destruction, if the deathless itself, which is eternal, is going to admit destruction.”

### 106d

**Socrates (continuing):** “I think everyone would agree that a god, the Form of Life itself, and anything else that is deathless, can never be destroyed.”

**Cebes:** “By Zeus, all human beings would agree, and even more so, I think, the gods.”

**Socrates:** “Since the deathless is also imperishable, must not the soul, if it happens to be deathless, also be imperishable?”

**Cebes:** “Necessarily.”

### 106e

**Socrates (continuing):** “Then when death comes upon a human being, the mortal part of him, it seems, dies, while the deathless part goes away whole and undestroyed, withdrawing before death.”

**Cebes:** “It appears so.”

**Socrates:** “So, Cebes, it is beyond all doubt that the soul is deathless and—”

### 107a

**Socrates (continuing):** “—imperishable, and our souls will truly exist in Hades.”

**Cebes:** “For my part, Socrates, I have nothing further to say against this, nor any way to distrust the arguments. But if Simmias here, or anyone else, has anything to say, it would be good not to keep silent. For I do not know to what other occasion someone could put off speaking or listening about such matters, if he wants to do either.”

**Simmias:** “I too have no further ground for distrust, given what has been said. Still, because of the greatness of the matters the arguments concern—”

### 107b

**Simmias (continuing):** “—and because I hold human weakness in low esteem, I am forced to retain some distrust in myself about what has been said.”

**Socrates:** “Not only is that well said, Simmias; even our first assumptions, if you trust them, must still be examined more clearly. If you work them through sufficiently, I think you will follow the argument as far as a human being can follow it. And if that itself becomes clear, you will seek nothing further.”

**Simmias:** “You speak truly.”

### 107c

**Socrates (continuing):** “But, gentlemen, we should reflect on this: if the soul is immortal, then it needs care not only for the time we call life, but for all time. The danger now may indeed seem terrible if someone neglects it. If death were release from everything, it would be a godsend for the wicked to die and be rid at once of their body and their wickedness along with their soul. But now, since the soul appears to be immortal, it can have no other—”

### 107d

**Socrates (continuing):** “—escape from evils or salvation except becoming as good and wise as possible. For the soul arrives in Hades carrying nothing else but its education and upbringing, things said to be of the greatest help or harm to the dead person right at the start of the journey there. The story goes like this: when each person dies, the guide who had been assigned to him in life tries to lead him to a certain place, where those gathered must undergo judgment and then travel to Hades with that guide who has been appointed to escort them from here to there.”

### 107e

**Socrates (continuing):** “Once they have arrived there and received what they must receive, and remained for the appointed time, another guide brings them back here, after long periods of time and many cycles.”

## Terminology and style notes

- **eidos / idea:** rendered as “Form,” including “the Beautiful itself,” “the Form of Life,” and “the Form of the even.”
- **aitia:** “cause”; the passage contrasts Forms as causes with material explanations such as color, shape, addition, and division.
- **ousia:** “being” or “proper being” where the text describes what a thing shares in.
- **psychē:** “soul”; “deathless” renders *athanatos* in the proof, while “immortal” appears where it functions as a familiar English term for the soul’s enduring state.
- The source’s constituted reading is followed: words marked `<del>` are not translated as main text.
