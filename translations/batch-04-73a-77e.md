# Batch 4: *Phaedo* 73a–77e

### 73a

**Socrates (continuing):** “—so in this respect too the soul seems to be immortal.”

Simmias interrupted: “But, Cebes, what are the proofs of this? Remind me; I do not remember them very well just now.”

“There is one especially fine argument,” said Cebes: “when people are questioned, if someone questions them well, they themselves say everything as it is. Yet they could not do this if knowledge and a correct account were not already within them. Then—”

### 73b

**Cebes (continuing):** “—if someone leads them to diagrams or something of that sort, they show most clearly that this is so.”

“If that does not convince you, Simmias,” Socrates said, “consider whether you might agree if you look at it this way. Are you doubtful about how what is called learning is recollection?”

“I am not doubtful, Socrates,” said Simmias, “but I want to experience just what the argument is about: to recollect. From nearly everything Cebes tried to say, I already remember and am convinced. All the same, I would be glad to hear now how you set out to make the case.”

### 73c

**Socrates (continuing):** “This is how. We surely agree that if someone recollects something, he must have known it at some earlier time.”

“Quite so,” he said.

“And do we also agree that when knowledge comes to be present in this particular way, it is recollection? What way do I mean? This: if someone, having perceived one thing—whether by seeing it, hearing it, or taking it in through some other sense—not only recognizes that thing but also thinks of another thing, knowledge of which is different and not the same, do we not rightly say that he has recollected the thing whose thought he has had?”

“What do you mean?”

“For example, the knowledge of a person is surely different from the knowledge of a lyre.”

“Of course.”

### 73d

**Socrates (continuing):** “You know, don’t you, that lovers have this experience: when they see a lyre, a cloak, or anything else the beloved is accustomed to use, they recognize the lyre and take in their thought the image of the boy whose lyre it is. That is recollection. Just as someone who sees Simmias often recollects Cebes, and there are countless other such cases.”

“Countless, by Zeus,” said Simmias.

### 73e

**Socrates (continuing):** “So this sort of thing is a kind of recollection, especially when someone has this experience about things that time and lack of attention had already made him forget?”

“Certainly,” he said.

“And what about this? On seeing a picture of a horse, can one recollect a person? Or, on seeing a picture of a lyre, recollect a person? And on seeing a picture of Simmias, recollect Cebes?”

“Certainly.”

### 74a

**Socrates (continuing):** “And on seeing a picture of Simmias, can one recollect Simmias himself?”

“One can,” he said.

“Then, in all these cases, recollection can arise both from similar things and from dissimilar ones?”

“Yes.”

“But when someone recollects something from similar things, must he not also have this thought: whether the thing is deficient in its similarity to what he recollected, or not?”

“Necessarily,” he said.

“Then consider whether matters stand this way. We say that there is something equal—not a piece of wood equal to another piece of wood, nor a stone equal to another stone, nor anything of that sort, but something different from all these, the Equal itself. Do we say that it is something, or nothing?”

### 74b

**Simmias:** “We certainly say it is something, by Zeus—something remarkable.”

**Socrates:** “And do we know what it is?”

**Simmias:** “We certainly do,” he said.

**Socrates:** “Where did we get the knowledge of it? Was it not from the things we were just mentioning? When we saw pieces of wood, stones, or other equal things, did we form the thought of that Equal, which is different from them? Or does it not seem different to you? Consider it this way too. Do not equal stones and pieces of wood sometimes appear equal to one person and not equal to another?”

**Simmias:** “Certainly.”

### 74c

**Socrates:** “And what of the Equal itself? Has it ever appeared to you unequal, or has Equality appeared to be Inequality?”

**Simmias:** “Never, Socrates.”

**Socrates:** “Then these equal things and the Equal itself are not the same.”

**Simmias:** “Not at all, Socrates, as it seems to me.”

**Socrates:** “And yet from these equal things—which are different from the Equal itself—you have thought of and acquired knowledge of it?”

**Simmias:** “You are entirely right.”

**Socrates:** “Whether it is similar to them or dissimilar?”

**Simmias:** “Yes.”

### 74d

**Socrates (continuing):** “It makes no difference. Whenever, from seeing one thing, you think of another, whether similar or dissimilar, that must necessarily have been recollection.”

“Certainly.”

“Now, do we experience anything like this with the equal things in pieces of wood and the things we just mentioned? Do they appear to us to be equal in the way the Equal itself is, or do they fall short of being like the Equal, or is there no difference?”

“They fall very far short,” he said.

**Socrates (continuing):** “Then we agree that whenever someone sees something and thinks, ‘This thing I am now seeing wants to be like some other thing that exists—”

### 74e

**Socrates (continuing):** “—but falls short and cannot be like it; it is inferior,’ the person having this thought must already have known the thing to which he says it is like, but less perfectly.”

“Necessarily.”

“Well then, have we too had this experience with equal things and the Equal itself, or have we not?”

“We certainly have.”

*Text note:* The source marks the word “equal” after “cannot be like it” as deleted; it is not included in the translated text.

### 75a

**Socrates (continuing):** “Then we must have known the Equal before that time when, on first seeing equal things, we thought that all these things strive to be like the Equal but fall short of it.”

“That is so.”

“And we also agree that we could not have thought of it, nor could we have been able to think of it, from any other source than seeing, touching, or some other sense. I mean all the senses together.”

“They all have the same effect, Socrates, as far as the argument is concerned.”

### 75b

**Socrates (continuing):** “But from the senses, surely, we must think that all the things we perceive strive to be what the Equal itself is, and are inferior to it. Or how do we put it?”

“That way.”

“So before we began to see, hear, and otherwise perceive, we must have acquired knowledge of the Equal itself—what it is—if we were going to refer the equal things we perceive through the senses to that, recognizing that all such things strive to be like it but are inferior to it.”

“That follows from what we said before, Socrates.”

“And did we not begin to see, hear, and have the other senses as soon as we were born?”

“Certainly.”

### 75c

**Socrates:** “But we say that we had to acquire knowledge of the Equal before those experiences?”

“Yes.”

“Then, it seems, we must have acquired it before we were born.”

“It seems so.”

“Now, if we acquired it before birth and were born possessing it, then we knew, both before birth and as soon as we were born, not only the Equal and the Greater and the Lesser, but everything of that kind. For our argument now concerns the Equal no more than the Beautiful itself, the Good itself, the Just, the Holy, and—just as I say—everything on which we stamp the phrase ‘what it is itself,’ both when we ask questions and when we give answers. So we must have acquired knowledge of all these things before birth.”

“That is so.”

### 75d

**Socrates (continuing):** “And if, each time, we acquired this knowledge without forgetting it, we would be born always knowing and would know throughout our lives. For knowing means this: to have acquired knowledge of something and not to have lost it. Or do we not call forgetting the loss of knowledge, Simmias?”

### 75e

**Simmias:** “Certainly, Socrates.”

“But, I suppose, if we acquired this knowledge before birth, then lost it when we were born, and later, by using our senses about those things, recover the knowledge we once had before, would what we call learning be recovering knowledge that belongs to us? And would we be right to call this recollection?”

“Certainly.”

**Socrates (continuing):** “For it appeared possible that, on perceiving something—seeing it, hearing it, or taking it in through another sense—we could think of something else from it, something we had forgotten, which it was connected with, either by being dissimilar or similar to it. So, as I say, one of two things must be true: either we were born already knowing these things and all of us know them throughout our lives, or those whom we say are learning later do nothing but recollect. Learning, then, would be recollection.”

“That is exactly how it is, Socrates.”

### 76a

**Socrates:** “Which do you choose, Simmias? Were we born knowing—”

### 76b

**Socrates (continuing):** “—or do we later recollect the things of which we had acquired knowledge earlier?”

**Simmias:** “I cannot choose just now, Socrates.”

**Socrates:** “Can you choose this, then, and tell me what you think about it? Is a person who knows something able or unable to give an account of what he knows?”

**Simmias:** “Necessarily able, Socrates.”

**Socrates:** “And do you think everyone can give an account of the things we were just talking about?”

**Simmias:** “I wish they could, but I am much more afraid that by this time tomorrow there will be no one left among human beings able to do so adequately.”

### 76c

**Socrates:** “Then you do not think, Simmias, that everyone knows these things?”

“Not at all.”

“So they recollect what they once learned?”

“Necessarily.”

“When did our souls acquire knowledge of them? Surely not since we became human beings.”

“No, certainly not.”

“So it was earlier.”

“Yes.”

“Then, Simmias, our souls existed earlier too, before they were in human form, apart from bodies, and they possessed understanding.”

“Unless, Socrates, we acquire this knowledge at the very moment we come to be. That time still remains as a possibility.”

### 76d

**Socrates:** “Very well, my friend. But at what other time do we lose it? For, as we just agreed, we are not born possessing it. Do we lose it at the same time that we acquire it? Or can you name another time?”

**Simmias:** “No, Socrates. I did not realize I was saying nothing.”

**Socrates:** “Then is this how matters stand for us, Simmias? If there exist the things we are always talking about—the Beautiful, the Good, and every such reality—and we refer everything we receive through the senses to these—”

### 76e

**Socrates (continuing):** “—discovering that they existed beforehand and were ours, and comparing the things we receive through the senses with them, then just as these things exist, so too our soul must exist before we are born. But if those things do not exist, would this argument have been made for nothing? Is it not equally necessary that these things exist and that our souls existed before we were born—and that if they do not, neither do our souls?”

**Simmias:** “Socrates, it seems to me that the necessity is remarkably strong, and the argument reaches a fine conclusion—”

### 77a

**Simmias (continuing):** “—that our soul existed before we were born in the same way as the reality you are now describing. Nothing seems clearer to me than that all such things exist as fully as possible: the Beautiful, the Good, and everything else you mentioned just now. And I think the proof is sufficient.”

**Socrates:** “And what does Cebes think? We must convince Cebes too.”

**Simmias:** “Sufficiently, I think, as far as I can tell—although Cebes is the most stubborn of men about distrusting arguments. Still, I think he has been sufficiently persuaded that our soul existed before we were born—”

### 77b

**Simmias (continuing):** “—but, Socrates, I do not think even he has been shown that it will still exist after we die. The concern Cebes raised just now remains: the common fear that when a person dies, the soul is scattered and that this is the end of its existence. What prevents it from coming to be, being formed from somewhere else, and existing before it reaches a human body, only then to end and perish itself when it has reached the body and leaves it?”

### 77c

**Cebes:** “You speak well, Simmias. It seems that only half of what must be proved has been shown: that our soul existed before we were born. We must add the proof that it will exist no less after we die than it did before we were born, if the demonstration is to be complete.”

**Socrates:** “It has been proved, Simmias and Cebes, provided you are willing to combine this argument with the one we agreed to earlier: that all living things come to be from the dead. For if the soul exists—”

### 77d

**Socrates (continuing):** “—beforehand, and if, when it goes into life and is born, it must come to be from nowhere other than death and being dead, how could it fail to exist after it dies, since it must come to be again? So what you are saying has indeed been proved. Still, I think you and Simmias would gladly examine this argument further, and that you are afraid of the children’s fear: that as the soul leaves the body, the wind might truly blow it away and scatter it—”

### 77e

**Socrates (continuing):** “—especially if someone happens to die not in calm weather but in a great storm.”

**Cebes:** Laughing, he said, “Then, Socrates, try to persuade us, as though we were afraid. Or rather, do not take it that we are afraid; perhaps there is a child within us who fears such things. Try to persuade him not to fear death as he fears bogeymen.”

**Socrates:** “Then we must sing an incantation over him every day until you have charmed him.”

## Terminology and style notes

- **Equal itself / the Equal:** Capitalized when the dialogue treats Equality as a reality distinct from sensible equal things. “Form” is not inserted where the Greek names the reality without that label.
- **recollection:** Used for *anamnēsis* and related verbs; “learning” remains distinct as the later recovery of prior knowledge.
- **reality:** Renders *ousia* at 76c in the phrase about the Beautiful, the Good, and “every such reality.”
- At 74e, the source marks a second “equal” (*ison*) after “cannot be like it” as deleted; it is omitted in the main text and recorded in the text note.
