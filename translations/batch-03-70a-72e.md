### 70a

**Phaedo:** After Socrates had said this, Cebes took up the conversation. “Socrates, everything else seems well said to me. But what you say about the soul leaves people very doubtful: perhaps, once it has left the body, it is nowhere anymore, but is destroyed and perishes on the very day a person dies. As soon as it leaves the body, it goes out like a breath or smoke, scatters, flies away, and is nowhere at all. If it were somewhere on its own, gathered together and freed from the evils you just described, there would be a great and fine hope, that what you say is true—”

### 70b

**Cebes (continuing):** “—but perhaps this needs no small measure of reassurance and confidence: that the soul exists after a person has died, and has some power and understanding.”

**Socrates:** “What you say is true, Cebes. So what should we do? Shall we discuss these very questions, and whether it is likely things are so or not?”

**Cebes:** “I would gladly hear what view you hold about them.”

**Socrates:** “I don’t think anyone who hears me now—”

### 70c

**Socrates (continuing):** “—not even a comic poet—would say that I’m chattering idly or making arguments about things that don’t concern us. If that seems right, then we should investigate.

“Let us examine the question this way: are the souls of people who have died in Hades, or are they not? There is an old account that we remember: souls go from here to there, and then return here again and are born from the dead. If this is so, and the living are born again from those who have died, what else could our souls be there—”

### 70d

**Socrates (continuing):** “—but souls? For surely they could not be born again if they did not exist. This would be adequate proof that this is so, if it became clear in fact that the living come from nowhere else than from the dead. But if that is not so, we would need another argument.”

**Cebes:** “Quite so.”

**Socrates:** “Then, if you want to understand this more easily, consider it not only in relation to human beings but also to all animals and plants, and, in a word, to everything that comes to be.”

### 70e

**Socrates (continuing):** “Let us see whether everything comes about this way: opposites from their opposites, wherever there is such a relation. For example, the beautiful is surely opposite to the ugly, the just to the unjust, and so with countless other things. Let us consider whether anything that has an opposite must come to be from nowhere else than from its opposite. For instance, when something becomes larger, must it first have been smaller and then become larger?”

### 71a

**Cebes:** “Yes.”

**Socrates:** “And when something becomes smaller, must it first have been larger and then become smaller?”

**Cebes:** “That is so.”

**Socrates:** “And the weaker comes from the stronger, the faster from the slower?”

**Cebes:** “Certainly.”

**Socrates:** “And if something becomes worse, does it come from something better? If it becomes more just, from something more unjust?”

**Cebes:** “Of course.”

**Socrates:** “Then have we established well enough that everything comes about this way, opposite things from opposites?”

**Cebes:** “Certainly.”

**Socrates:** “And is there also this feature in them: between every pair of opposites there are two processes of coming-to-be—”

### 71b

**Socrates (continuing):** “—one from one side to the other, and then again from the other side to the first? Between the larger and the smaller, for example, there are growth and decline. We call one growing and the other diminishing.”

**Cebes:** “Yes.”

**Socrates:** “So too, separating and combining, cooling and heating, and everything else in this way. Even if we do not use the terms in every case, in fact it must be so throughout: each comes to be from the other, and there is a process by which each comes to be the other.”

**Cebes:** “Certainly.”

### 71c

**Socrates:** “What then? Is there something opposite to being alive, just as being asleep is opposite to being awake?”

**Cebes:** “Certainly.”

**Socrates:** “What is it?”

**Cebes:** “Being dead.”

**Socrates:** “And these come to be from one another, if they are opposites, and the processes of coming-to-be between them are two, since there are two opposites?”

**Cebes:** “Of course.”

**Socrates:** “I’ll tell you about one pair of the two I just mentioned, and about its processes. You tell me about the other. I mean sleeping and waking: waking comes to be from sleeping—”

### 71d

**Socrates (continuing):** “—and from being awake, falling asleep; and their processes of coming-to-be are, for one, falling asleep, and for the other, waking up. Is that clear enough?”

**Cebes:** “Quite clear.”

**Socrates:** “Now tell me in the same way about life and death. Don’t you say that being dead is the opposite of being alive?”

**Cebes:** “I do.”

**Socrates:** “And do they come to be from one another?”

**Cebes:** “Yes.”

**Socrates:** “Then what comes to be from what is alive?”

**Cebes:** “What is dead.”

**Socrates:** “And what comes from what is dead?”

**Cebes:** “We must agree that it is what is alive.”

**Socrates:** “So the living things and the living come to be from the dead, Cebes?”

### 71e

**Cebes:** “It seems so.”

**Socrates:** “Then our souls exist in Hades.”

**Cebes:** “It seems so.”

**Socrates:** “And of the two processes of coming-to-be involved here, one is certainly clear: dying, surely—isn’t it?”

**Cebes:** “Certainly.”

**Socrates:** “Then what shall we do? Shall we fail to provide the opposite process of coming-to-be, leaving nature limping on this side? Or must we provide some process opposite to dying?”

**Cebes:** “We must, surely.”

**Socrates:** “What is it?”

**Cebes:** “Coming back to life.”

**Socrates:** “Well then, if coming back to life exists, from the dead—”

### 72a

**Socrates (continuing):** “—this would be the process by which the living come to be: coming back to life?”

**Cebes:** “Certainly.”

**Socrates:** “So we agree in this respect too: the living have come to be from the dead no less than the dead from the living. And if that is so, it seemed to provide sufficient proof that the souls of those who have died must exist somewhere, from which they come back to life.”

**Cebes:** “That seems necessary, Socrates, on the basis of what we have agreed.”

**Socrates:** “Then consider it this way, Cebes: I think we were right to agree to these things. If opposites did not continually give rise to one another in turn—”

### 72b

**Socrates (continuing):** “—as though moving around in a circle, but coming-to-be went in a straight line from one opposite to its contrary and never turned back again or curved around toward the other, you know that everything would eventually take on the same character, undergo the same condition, and stop coming to be.”

**Cebes:** “What do you mean?”

**Socrates:** “It is not hard to understand what I mean. Suppose falling asleep existed, but waking did not repay it by coming to be from the sleeper. You know that in the end everything—”

### 72c

**Socrates (continuing):** “—would make the story of Endymion pointless, and he would be nowhere to be seen, because everything else would have suffered the same fate as him: sleeping. And if everything combined but nothing separated, Anaxagoras’ saying ‘all things together’ would soon come true.

“The same applies, my dear Cebes, if everything that shared in life were to die, and, once dead, remained in that state and did not come back to life. Wouldn’t everything inevitably end up—”

### 72d

**Socrates (continuing):** “—dead, with nothing alive? For if the living came to be from other things, while the living died, what could prevent everything from being used up in death?”

**Cebes:** “Nothing, I think, Socrates. You seem to me to be entirely right.”

**Socrates:** “Yes, Cebes, I think this is absolutely the case. We have not been deceived in agreeing to these things. Coming back to life truly exists; the living come to be from the dead; and the souls of those who have died—”

### 72e

**Socrates (continuing):** “—exist.”

The Greek text marks the following words as deleted, so they are not translated here: “and for the good ones it is better, for the bad ones worse.”

**Cebes:** “And indeed, Socrates, even according to that other argument you often make, if it is true—that learning is in fact nothing other than recollection—then surely we must have learned at some earlier time what we now recollect.”
## Terminology and style notes

- **ψυχή (*psychē*):** “soul,” including in Cebes’ concern about whether it survives bodily death.
- **γένεσις / γίγνεσθαι:** rendered contextually as “process of coming-to-be,” “come to be,” or “be born”; here the cyclical process is central to the argument.
- **ἀναβιώσκεσθαι:** “coming back to life,” expressing the return from death to life.
- **ἐναντίον:** “opposite”; used for the opposed states and qualities from which each other arises.
- **φρόνησις:** “understanding” in Cebes’ description of the soul’s capacities.
- **λόγος:** “account” or “argument,” according to context; no global rendering is proposed here.
- The source has a deletion at 72e after “the souls of the dead exist” (“and for the good ones it is better, for the bad ones worse”); it is excluded from the translated text as deleted material.
