# Plato, *Phaedo* — Batch 9 (108a–115e)

### 108a

**Socrates (as reported by Phaedo):** “So the journey is not as Aeschylus’ Telephus describes it. He says that a single road leads to Hades. It seems to me that it is neither simple nor one. Otherwise there would be no need for guides: no one would miss the way if there were only one. As it is, it seems to have many forks and crossroads. I infer this from the sacrifices and rites here. The orderly and wise soul follows its guide and is not ignorant of what lies ahead. But a soul attached to the body by desire, as I said earlier, and long distraught about that and the visible region—”

### 108b

**Socrates (continuing):** “—after much resistance and suffering, is led away forcibly and with difficulty by the spirit appointed to guide it. When it arrives where the other souls are, if it is impure and has done something of that sort—if it has been involved in unjust killings or has committed other such deeds, kin to these and the work of souls of the same kind—everyone flees and turns aside from it. No one is willing to travel with it or be its guide.”

### 108c

**Socrates (continuing):** “It wanders by itself in complete perplexity until a certain time has passed. When that time comes, it is carried by necessity to the dwelling suited to it. But a soul that has passed its life in purity and moderation finds gods as companions and guides, and each soul settles in the place appropriate to it. There are many wonderful places on the earth itself, and the earth is neither of the kind nor of the size that people accustomed to speak about it suppose, as I have been persuaded by someone.”

### 108d

**Simmias:** “How do you mean this, Socrates? I too have heard a great deal about the earth, but not what has persuaded you. I would gladly hear it.”

**Socrates:** “Simmias, I do not think Glaucus’ skill would be needed to describe what it is actually like. To describe it as true seems harder than Glaucus’ art; and perhaps I could not do it. Even if I knew how, I think my life would not be long enough for so lengthy an account. Still, nothing prevents me from telling you the shape of the earth as I believe it to be—”

### 108e

**Socrates (continuing):** “—and its places, as I believe they are.”

**Simmias:** “But these things too will suffice.”

**Socrates:** “Well, I am persuaded first that, if the earth is spherical and lies at the center of the heavens, it needs neither air to keep it from falling nor any other such necessity—”

### 109a

**Socrates (continuing):** “—the likeness of the heavens to themselves on every side and the earth’s own balance are enough to hold it. For a balanced thing placed at the center of something uniform cannot incline more in one direction than another; remaining evenly poised, it will stay without leaning. This is the first thing I am persuaded of.”

**Simmias:** “And rightly so.”

**Socrates:** “Further, I am persuaded that the earth is immensely large, and that we live—”

### 109b

**Socrates (continuing):** “—from the Phasis to the Pillars of Heracles occupy only a small part of it, living around the sea as ants or frogs live around a pond. Many other people live elsewhere, in many such regions. For there are hollows of every kind and size all around the earth, into which water, mist, and air have flowed together. The earth itself lies pure in the pure heaven where the stars are, the heaven most people who discuss these things call ‘aether.’”

### 109c

**Socrates (continuing):** “These things are its sediment, flowing continually into the hollows of the earth. We live in those hollows without realizing it, and suppose we live on the upper surface of the earth. It is as if someone living at the bottom in the middle of the sea thought he lived on the sea’s surface: seeing the sun and the other stars through the water, he would take the sea for the sky, but—”

### 109d

**Socrates (continuing):** “—because of his slowness and weakness he would never have reached the sea’s edge or seen, after coming out of the water and raising his head into this region, how much purer and more beautiful it is than the place where he lives. Nor would he have heard from anyone who had seen it. We have undergone the same thing: living in a hollow of the earth, we suppose we live on its upper surface and call the air ‘the heaven,’ as though the stars moved through it. But the truth is the same—”

### 109e

**Socrates (continuing):** “—because of our weakness and slowness, we cannot pass through the air to its outermost limit. For if someone reached its edge, or grew wings and flew up, he would raise his head and see what is there—just as fish here, when they leap up from the sea, see what is here. If his nature could endure the sight, he would recognize that this is the true heaven, the true light—”

### 110a

**Socrates (continuing):** “—and the true earth.”

**Socrates:** “For this earth here, its stones, and the whole region are spoiled and eaten away, as things in the sea are by salt. Nothing worthwhile grows in the sea, and nothing there is, so to speak, fully formed. There are clefts, sand, immeasurable mud, and mire wherever there is land; none of it is in any way worthy to be compared with the beauties among us. But those things there would appear to differ from ours by far more still—”

### 110b

**Socrates (continuing):** “—if it is pleasant to tell a story, Simmias, it is worth hearing what things are on the earth beneath the heaven.”

**Simmias:** “By all means, Socrates. We would gladly hear this story.”

**Socrates:** “It is said, my friend, that the earth itself looks like a ball made of twelve pieces of leather, variegated and divided into colors. The colors here are samples of those colors, which painters use. There the whole earth is made of such colors—”

### 110c

**Socrates (continuing):** “—and of colors far brighter and purer than these. One part is purple, wonderful in beauty; another is golden; another is whiter than gypsum or snow. It is composed in the same way of the other colors, and of colors more numerous and beautiful than any we have seen. Even its hollows, filled with water and air—”

### 110d

**Socrates (continuing):** “—have a kind of color of their own, gleaming amid the variety of the other colors, so that the whole presents a single continuous play of color. Since it is like this, whatever grows there grows correspondingly: trees, flowers, and fruits. The mountains too, and the stones, are more beautiful in their smoothness, transparency, and color. The stones we value here—carnelian, jasper, emerald—”

### 110e

**Socrates (continuing):** “—and all such things. There, every stone is like these, and even more beautiful. The reason is that those stones are pure, not eaten away or spoiled as ours are by rot and saltwater. The same is true of the precious stones and all the things we prize: they are fragments of the stones there. And the earth itself is adorned with all these things, and also with gold, silver, and—”

### 111a

**Socrates (continuing):** “—the other such things. They are visible there, for they are numerous, large, and spread everywhere over the earth, so that it is a sight for blessed spectators. There are many other living creatures on it, and human beings too: some dwell inland, others around the air as we dwell around the sea, and others on islands near the mainland, with air flowing around them. In a word, what water and sea are for our use, that there—”

### 111b

**Socrates (continuing):** “—is air for them, and what air is for us, aether is for them. Their seasons are so tempered that they are free from disease and live much longer than people here. Their sight, hearing, thought, and every such capacity surpass ours by the same measure as air surpasses water and aether surpasses air in purity. They have sacred groves and temples in which gods truly dwell; they hear their voices, receive oracles and visions of them, and converse with them face to face—”

### 111c

**Socrates (continuing):** “They see the sun, moon, and stars as they truly are, and their blessedness in every other respect follows from this. The earth as a whole is like this, and the regions around it. But many hollows lie all around it. Some are deeper and more open than the region we inhabit; some are deeper but have a smaller chasm than ours—”

### 111d

**Socrates (continuing):** “—and some are shallower than our region but wider. All these hollows are pierced in many places beneath the earth and connected with one another; they have narrower and wider passages through which much water flows from one to another, as if into mixing bowls. Beneath the earth are immeasurable sizes of everlasting rivers, hot and cold waters, much fire and great rivers of fire, and many streams of liquid mud, both purer and more muddy—”

### 111e

**Socrates (continuing):** “—like the mud rivers in Sicily before a lava flow, and the lava stream itself. Each region is filled by these streams as the current reaches it. All these things are moved up and down by a kind of oscillation within the earth. This oscillation has a natural cause of the following sort.”

### 112a

**Socrates (continuing):** “One of the earth’s chasms is exceptionally large and pierced right through the whole earth. This is the chasm Homer describes as ‘far away, where beneath the earth lies the deepest pit.’ He and many other poets have elsewhere called it Tartarus. All the rivers flow together into this chasm and flow out from it again. Each becomes the kind of stream that corresponds to the region through which it flows.”

### 112b

**Socrates (continuing):** “The reason all the streams flow out from there and flow back in is that this liquid mass has no bottom or base. It hangs and surges up and down, and the air and wind around it do the same. The wind follows the water whenever it rushes toward either side of the earth. Just as the breath of those breathing is always flowing out and in, so there the wind, rising and falling with the water, produces terrible and immense gusts as it flows in and out—”

### 112c

**Socrates (continuing):** “—When the water withdraws into the place called the lower region, it flows through the earth’s passages into the streams in that region and fills them, as people pump water into reservoirs. When it leaves that region and rushes this way, it fills the streams here in turn. Once these are full, they flow through channels and through the earth, each arriving at the places to which its route leads, making seas, lakes, rivers, and springs. From there they sink again beneath the earth—”

### 112d

**Socrates (continuing):** “—some traveling around longer and more numerous regions, others fewer and shorter ones, and they flow back into Tartarus, some much lower than the place from which they were pumped, others only a little. All flow back beneath the point of outflow; some opposite the place from which they emerged, others on the same side. Some make a complete circuit, winding around the earth once or many times, like snakes, and sink as far down as they can before flowing back in.”

### 112e

**Socrates (continuing):** “They can descend on either side as far as the middle, but no farther. The region on either side slopes upward for both streams. Among the other rivers are many great and varied currents. Among all these there happen to be four particular streams. The largest and outermost, flowing in a circle, is called Oceanus. Opposite it and flowing in the contrary direction is Acheron, which runs through deserted places—”

### 113a

**Socrates (continuing):** “—and, running beneath the earth, reaches the Acherusian Lake. The souls of most of the dead arrive there. After remaining for their appointed periods, some longer and some shorter, they are sent back again into the births of living things. A third river flows out between these, and near its outlet it falls into a great place burning with much fire, making a lake larger than our sea, boiling with water and mud. From there—”

### 113b

**Socrates (continuing):** “—it flows in a circle, turbid and muddy, winds around the earth, and reaches other places, including the outer edge of the Acherusian Lake. It does not mix with the lake’s water. After winding around many times beneath the earth, it empties into the lower part of Tartarus. This is the river they call Pyriphlegethon; its branches throw up fragments of lava wherever they reach the earth. Opposite it, a fourth river first falls into a fearful and savage place, as the story goes, all dark blue in color—”

### 113c

**Socrates (continuing):** “—which they call the Stygian region, and the lake formed by the river’s flow is called Styx. After falling into it and taking on terrible powers in the water, the river sinks beneath the earth, circles around, and flows in the opposite direction to Pyriphlegethon. It meets that river from the opposite side at the Acherusian Lake. Its water too mixes with no other; after circling around, it empties into Tartarus opposite Pyriphlegethon. The poets call it Cocytus.”

### 113d

**Socrates (continuing):** “When the dead arrive at the place to which each is carried by his guide, they are first judged: those who have lived well and reverently, and those who have not. Those judged to have lived an ordinary life travel to Acheron. They board the vessels provided for them and arrive at the lake. There they dwell and are purified; they pay the penalty for any wrong they have done and are released, while for their good deeds each receives honors according to their deserts—”

### 113e

**Socrates (continuing):** “—but those judged incurable because of the magnitude of their offenses—those who committed many great acts of sacrilege, unjust and lawless murders, or other such crimes—are thrown by their proper fate into Tartarus, from which they never emerge.”

### 114a

**Socrates (continuing):** “Those who committed great but curable offenses—for example, doing violence to a father or mother in anger and then living the rest of their lives in remorse, or becoming killers in some other such way—must also fall into Tartarus. But after they have fallen there and spent a year, the wave casts them out: the murderers along Cocytus, those who attacked their parents along Pyriphlegethon. When they are carried to the Acherusian Lake, they cry out and call upon the people they killed or wronged, begging and pleading—”

### 114b

**Socrates (continuing):** “—with those people to let them step out into the lake and receive them. If they persuade them, they come out and their sufferings end. If not, they are carried back again to Tartarus, and from there into the rivers; they suffer this until they persuade those they wronged. The judges imposed this penalty on them.”

### 114c

**Socrates (continuing):** “Those judged to have excelled in reverent living are released and freed from these regions within the earth, as from prisons. They arrive above in the pure dwelling and settle on the earth. Among these, those who have purified themselves sufficiently through philosophy live entirely without bodies for all the time to come, and arrive at dwellings even more beautiful than these. It is not easy to describe them, and the time we have now is not enough.”

“For all these reasons, Simmias, we must do everything we can during life to share in virtue and wisdom. The prize is beautiful, and the hope is great.”

### 114d

“A sensible man should not insist that these things are exactly as I have described them. But that this, or something of the sort, is true of our souls and their dwellings—since the soul appears to be immortal—seems to me fitting and worth the risk for someone who believes it to be so. For the risk is beautiful. One should repeat such things to oneself as a spell. That is why I have drawn out this story for so long. For these reasons one should be confident about his soul—”

### 114e

**Socrates (continuing):** “—the man who, during life, has let the other pleasures of the body and its adornments go, as things alien to him, and has judged them more likely to do harm than good; while he has devoted himself to the pleasures of learning, and adorned his soul not with an alien ornament but with its own: self-control, justice, courage, freedom, and truth. Such a man waits for his journey to Hades.”

### 115a

**Socrates (continuing):** “You, then, Simmias and Cebes and all the rest, will each make your journey later, at some other time. But my own fate, as a tragic poet might say, is calling me now, and it is almost time for me to turn to the bath. I think it is better to drink the drug after bathing, and not to trouble the women with washing my body when I am dead.”

### 115b

**Crito:** “All right, Socrates. What instructions do you have for me or the others about your children or anything else? What could we do to please you most?”

**Socrates:** “Just what I always say, Crito; nothing new: if you take care of yourselves, whatever you do will be welcome to me, to my family, and to yourselves, even if you do not promise it now. But if you neglect yourselves and are unwilling to live, following the tracks of what we have just said and what we said earlier—”

### 115c

**Socrates (continuing):** “—then even if you make many solemn promises now, you will accomplish nothing more.”

**Crito:** “We will try to do this. But how should we bury you?”

**Socrates:** “However you wish, if you can catch me and I do not escape you.”

He laughed quietly, looked toward us, and said: “Men, I cannot persuade Crito that I am this Socrates here, the one now conversing with you and arranging each part of the discussion—”

### 115d

**Socrates (continuing):** “—but he thinks I am the one he will see shortly as a corpse, and he asks how he should bury me. I have spent a long time explaining that, after I drink the drug, I will no longer remain with you, but go away to some blessed state of the fortunate. It seems to me he thinks I say all that for nothing, to comfort both you and myself. So give Crito a pledge on my behalf, the opposite pledge to the one he gave the judges. He pledged that I would remain; you pledge that I will not remain after I die, but will go away—”

### 115e

**Socrates (continuing):** “—so that Crito will bear it more easily, and when he sees my body being burned or buried he will not be angry on my behalf, as though I were suffering terrible things. Nor should he say at my funeral that he is laying out Socrates, carrying him out, or burying him. Know this well, my dear Crito: speaking badly is not only a fault in itself; it also does harm to souls.”

**Socrates (continuing):** “But you should be confident and say that you are burying my body, and bury it—”

## Terminology and style notes

- **φάρμακον (*pharmakon*):** “drug” at 115b, where Socrates discusses when to drink it, following the glossary.
- **δαίμων (*daimōn*):** “spirit” or “guide,” depending on context, for the appointed escort of each soul.
- At 114e–115a the source deletes the words equivalent to “as one who will go when fate calls”; they are excluded from the translation.
- At 114d the source has a section milestone without a `resp="Stephanus"` attribute; it is included as a heading because it is a numbered section boundary in the source.
